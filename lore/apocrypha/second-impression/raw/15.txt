%%TITLE The Twelve-Pipe Fugue in Retrograde

They found the reel in the flooded basement annex of the conservatory in late October, sealed inside a rusted brass canister stamped #m[1872-TT1 — TWELVE PIPES —] #m[48s].

I am an organist and a musicologist, not an archivist of ghosts. When the dean asked me to transcribe the tape for the winter recital program, I expected a Victorian curiosity—one of those clever, bloodless mirror-canons minor German theorists used to write to prove they had read Bach's _Musical Offering_.

On paper, that is almost what it was.

A single twelve-note theme played on dry, reedy organ pipes, accompanied simultaneously by its own exact retrograde—the same melody played backward from the final bar toward the first—so that the two voices crossed cleanly at the twenty-fourth second and resolved on a bare, unisons-only cadence at forty-eight.

Only when I timed the magnetic ribbon against my brass stopwatch in the practice loft did I notice the fault.

The tape ran forty-nine seconds.

In the closing bar—written plainly for six beats on the yellowing manuscript sheet tucked inside the canister—the retrograde voice arrived one count late. Six beats passed, the forward line closed cleanly on the tonic, and then a seventh beat entered alone, slightly flat, hanging in the damp air of the loft like a footstep on a stair after everyone else has left the building.

I told myself the oxide ribbon had stretched in the flood. To settle the question, I booked the old limestone chapel on the bluff above the gorge for civil twilight on a Friday evening—the only room in the county with a tracker-action positive organ whose twelve principal pipes matched the dry, narrow temperament of the tape.

%%CANON

The chapel was empty and cold. Through the lancet windows, November mist was rising off the dark river bottoms. I set the manuscript on the music desk, pulled the single eight-foot reed stop and a quiet sixteen-foot pedal, and began to play both hands at once—right hand forward, left hand in retrograde.

At the twelfth second, the acoustics of the chapel changed.

A limestone nave forty feet wide should return a dry reed tone in less than a tenth of a second. Instead, my chords came back nearly half a second late—deep, resonant, and slightly warm, as if the stone walls behind the choir stalls had opened out into an unroofed amphitheater or a cistern cut deep into the bluff. Beneath the floorboards, a steady sixty-nine-hertz hum began to answer my pedal note, vibrating cleanly up through the soles of my shoes.

I did not stop. A musician does not break off a canon in the middle of the bridge.

At the twenty-fourth second, where the forward melody and its mirror image crossed in the center of the keyboard, the air in the choir loft grew unexpectedly warm and smelled of bruised quince and damp alder pollen—six months out of season. Up in the high stonework beside the east window, I heard three faint, dry clicks—like ceramic striking stone—and then complete stillness, as if an audience I could not see had leaned forward in the dark pews to hear how the cadence would resolve.

I reached the closing bar. I counted six beats aloud, lifted both hands cleanly from the manuals, and closed the stop.

The organ pipes went silent.

And then, half a breath later, the chapel itself played the seventh beat.

It did not come from the loft. It came from the far end of the dark nave, low and unhurried, carrying the exact narrow temperament of the 1872 reel—as if a thirteenth pipe had been waiting inside the limestone since the chapel was built for someone careless enough to supply the first forty-eight seconds.

I gathered my manuscript off the desk with shaking fingers. Stuck to the back of the final page, sewn neatly along the left margin with waxed yellow linen thread I had not noticed in the conservatory basement, was a single leaf of older rag paper bearing three unmarked vocal lines—_Steward_, _Chorister_, _Stranger_—and a dry pencilled rubric at the bottom noting that the downstroke had been held, and that the dismissal was not yet due.

I locked the organ console, left the chapel by the side vestry door without looking back down the nave, and walked home through the fog with my hands jammed deep into my pockets, counting my own footsteps in sixes—and trying very hard not to hear the seventh step fall behind me on the wet gravel.

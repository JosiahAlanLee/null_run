#dept[Null Run --- Works \& Descent / Stores Department (September 1953)]

== I. Pump Station No. 4

The trouble began with a thrust bearing, which is the proper sort of trouble for a joiner to have, because a thrust bearing has a part number and a shelf in Stores.

K. Marek stood at the iron grating of Pump Station No. 4 on the morning of 4 September 1953, holding Installation Record L1-05 in one gloved hand and a brass calipers gauge in the other. The paper was clipped to the pump housing with a rusted spring clamp. It stated, in neat agency typescript, that Pump Station No. 4 had been installed and made operational in 1895, that all foundry marks confirmed 1895, and that persons requiring the pump were advised the station was not presently served.

Marek crouched beside the lower casing and wiped the red ferric brine off the cast-iron flange with a cotton rag. The brine was thick, cold as iron in winter—minus 2.4 degrees by the pocket gauge—and smelled of wet rust. Underneath the smear, the raised foundry numerals stood clean and unmistakable in the casting: *1872*.

In the corner of the pump housing, someone had pencilled #m[L4-05] directly onto the paint in an older, squared-off hand.

Marek straightened up slowly. His left shoulder had a dry, mechanical stiffness that worsened whenever he stood too long near the red brine, as if the collar joint were short a shim. He checked his jacket pocket for the receipt for his spare bearing, Part 214-Q. The receipt was still there, folded in quarters, stamped #m[WORKS & DESCENT —] #m[ALLOWANCE VERIFIED]. Yet when he had gone to the stores hatch at six o'clock to draw the bearing, the clerk had informed him—with every courtesy—that Part 214-Q had already been returned to Stores on 3 September under his own badge number.

"Under what signature?" Marek had asked.

"The line is initialled," the clerk had replied, turning the ledger slightly so Marek could see the column without touching the page. "Badge NR-311-R."

"My badge is 311-M," Marek had said. "M for Marek. Joiner, Provisional."

"The ribbon on the intake machine jammed in '52," the clerk had said gently. "An overstruck M frequently reads as an R on the carbon copy. If you wish to contest the classification, you may file a petition at Counter 2. For your comfort, the pump is not presently served in any case."

== II. The Breakroom Wall

Marek walked back along the East Service Corridor to draft his petition in the breakroom. The corridor lamps burned steady—electric bulbs behind wire cages, though the brass gas-jet brackets of the 1870s still protruded from the masonry six inches below every electric conduit, capped with tarnished lead plugs.

At the intake spigot beside the third pilaster, Marek filled a tin cup to rinse the brine from his calipers. Notice L1-04 was riveted above the tap, announcing that the draw had been reclassified to the western glacial melt feed at pH 7.2 for the comfort of personnel. Marek dipped a litmus strip from his apron pocket into the tin cup. It turned a flat, unmistakable slate-blue: lake water, pH 7.8. He dried the strip between his thumb and forefinger and folded it into his notebook beside the receipt for Part 214-Q.

The breakroom was empty when he arrived. The air inside was completely still—no draft from the wall vent, no sound from the corridor once the latch clicked shut. On the west wall hung Inspection Tag L1-06, issued by the Internal Audit Bureau, assigning the wall Rating A and certifying the rock as sound granite with no fractures.

Marek set his calipers on the wooden table and looked at the west wall. Even from six feet away, the limestone flow-fractures ran floor to ceiling in pale diagonal seams. Three small marine shells sat embedded in the grey stone beside the electrical conduit.

He opened his notebook, unscrewed his fountain pen, and began to write his petition to the Internal Audit Bureau. Halfway down the page he stopped and looked at his own allowance slip, which he had laid beside the ink bottle.

At the top of the slip, his breakroom allowance was entered under #m[CLASS: EQUIPMENT]. On the second slip, issued by Stores for his winter boots and coat, his effects were filed under #m[CLASS: PERSONNEL].

Marek sat for a long time with the pen poised over the paper. He was not frightened; fright is an inefficient way to deal with a filing clerk. What bothered him was the arithmetic. If a man's boots are filed as personnel and his lunch allowance is filed as equipment, and his spare thrust bearing—Part 214-Q, which is supposed to sit in a pump housing—is also entered on a forge marking-plate tally of 206 pieces that came up one piece short at the left collar, then someone in Records had merged two drawers that had no business sharing a tab.

He looked down at his own left wrist. Underneath the cuff of his canvas work-shirt, where the cold always settled first, his pulse was slow and oddly regular—not the quick, uneven flutter of a man who had walked three flights up from the sluice, but a steady, dry thirty-five beats to the minute, like a small brass ratchet ticking inside a wooden box.

He crossed out a word on his petition—#m[safe]—and replaced it with _one entry_, folded the sheet cleanly into thirds, and walked back out to Counter 2.

== III. Counter 2

The clerk at Counter 2 was not the man who had been on duty at six o'clock.

This one was tall and narrow-shouldered, wearing a clean grey shirt and a dark tie knotted slightly too high against the collar. He sat motionless behind the brass grille with both hands laid flat on the ledger, his elbows angled slightly outward in a stiff, careful posture. The overhead lamp behind the grille had faded down to a low, steady amber—no flicker, merely dim—so the clerk's face stayed mostly in shadow, wrapped closely in what looked at first glance like warm, pale packing-paper creased along the bridge of the nose.

Marek did not stare. It is bad manners to stare at a clerk on a double shift.

"I have three petitions," Marek said, sliding the folded papers under the brass bars. "Pump Station No. 4, the East Service draw allowance, and the breakroom classification. And I would like to inspect Part 214-Q, if it is actually in the cage."

The clerk did not speak. He drew the three petitions toward himself with long, careful fingers, stamped each one in violet ink (#m[ACKNOWLEDGED — NUMBERED — DEFERRED]), and placed them in a wire basket that was already empty.

Then he reached beneath the counter and set a small greased-paper parcel on the blotter.

Marek untied the twine. Inside lay a tarnished thrust bearing stamped #m[214-Q], its inner race packed with a pale, dry chlorite shim and a thin thread of green fibrous grit that smelled faintly of cold freshwater silt. Beside the bearing lay a blank receipt form— Form 9-B—with two boxes printed at the bottom: one for the name of the bearer, and one for the class of the article.

"The bearing is mine," Marek said quietly. "Though I never shimmed it with chlorite."

The clerk tilted his paper-wrapped head a fraction of an inch to the left, as if listening to something inside Marek's coat rather than to his voice. From behind the counter came no sound of breathing at all—only, very faintly, the dry rasp of heavy linen shifting against a wooden stool.

Marek picked up the brass pen from the inkwell. He signed #m[K. Marek] cleanly on the bearer's line. When his pen reached the classification box, he hesitated, looking from the #m[EQUIPMENT] stamp on his allowance card to the #m[PERSONNEL] stamp on his coat chit, and then up at the quiet clerk sitting behind the grille in a clean shirt and tie.

He left the classification box blank.

"For your comfort," the clerk's stamp read when he pressed it to the carbon copy and slid the receipt back through the bars:

%%BLOCK doc

> DISPOSITION: IN SERVICE --- AWAITING ASSESSMENT.

Marek put the parcel in his coat pocket, nodded politely to the clerk, and walked back down the corridor toward the pump stairs, careful not to let his hobnails ring against the stone.

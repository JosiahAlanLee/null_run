%%TITLE The Footnote That Outweighed the Folio

The book arrived at my bindery on a sleeting Tuesday in November, wrapped in oiled canvas and tied with butcher's twine that smelled faintly of cold iron and damp alder.

There was no cover letter—only a stamped requisition slip from an estates liquidator I had never heard of, requesting that the spine be re-sewn, the signatures pressed, and the whole returned before the quarter closed. At the bottom of the slip, in neat violet ink, someone had added a single instruction: _Do not brush the lower margin._

I untied the canvas on my oak bench beneath the green-shaded lamp.

It was a privately printed 1898 critical edition of a two-act verse drama whose title page had been water-stained down to a pale, illegible ochre. The play itself occupied twelve thin leaves of laid rag paper. Act I was polite, faded decadence—three courtiers waiting in a lakeside pavilion for a host who would not arrive until the masks were laid aside. Act II broke off halfway down the ninth leaf, replaced by a dense lattice of editorial footnotes set in six-point Caslon.

The footnotes ran for two hundred and fourteen pages.

I have bound university theses, parish registers, and private occult pamphlets for thirty-five years, and I know the madness of a commentator who refuses to let the text speak for itself. Yet this apparatus was different. The notes did not merely gloss the play; they argued with one another in three separate hands, citing three mutually exclusive locations for the theater where the play had supposedly been staged—a subterranean gallery beneath twelve hundred metres of fresh lake water, a subglacial vault cut below seven hundred metres of ice, and a sealed limestone cavern where no daylight fell at any hour.

And buried inside Note 114—a footnote attached to a footnote explaining why the curtain cue was missing—sat a single loose leaf of heavier rag stock that had never been trimmed to the folio's edges. It was stamped #m[1953-B] in the upper-right corner, and its lower margin carried a thin, damp ridge of sweet-smelling yellow dust.

When I lifted that single leaf off the bench to check its watermark, my wrist buckled.

I set it down so hard the paste-pot rattled. Paper does not weigh that much. A four-page signature of heavy laid rag weighs less than two ounces. I carried the single leaf across the shop to my cast-iron beam scale—the same balance I use for parcel post—checked the beam against two one-pound brass tares, and laid #m[1953-B] flat on the pan.

I had to slide both brass poises all the way out to the extreme end of the beam before the iron tongue settled dead level.

Forty-one pounds, eight ounces. For one sheet of paper that bent lightly between my fingers when I picked it back up.

I stood in the quiet shop for a full minute, listening to the sleet tap against the front glass. I did not throw the leaf in the stove. A bookbinder is a creature of habit, and habit is a remarkably sturdy armor against the impossible. Instead, I threaded a heavy needle with waxed yellow linen—the only cord in my drawer strong enough to hold the gathering without tearing the gutter—and began to sew the signatures back onto the tapes.

As I worked, I made the mistake of reading the final footnote at the bottom of page 214.

It was not in the 1898 typeface. It was set in a crisp, modern sans-serif, citing a binder's receipt dated the very Tuesday I was sitting at my bench. It noted that the bookbinder had weighed the loose leaf on a cast-iron scale, confirmed the beam against two one-pound tares, and proceeded to bind the folio anyway—_thereby completing the second act without speaking a single line aloud_.

I stopped with the needle halfway through the kettle-stitch.

Outside the rain-streaked glass of my shopfront, beneath the steady amber glow of the streetlamp, a tall figure in a dark pleated coat stood motionless on the wet brick pavement. They carried no luggage. They were facing away from my window, their back turned squarely to the street, showing only a small square of stitched pale cloth pinned neatly between the shoulder blades.

I did not pull the needle back out. I tied off the yellow linen thread, pressed the boards firmly under the iron nipping-press until the paste set, and laid the finished book flat on the counter. On the shipping tag I wrote the only honest weight I could swear to, left the shop lamp burning steady, and locked the front door from the inside—knowing perfectly well that the folio on my bench was already heavier than the table holding it up.

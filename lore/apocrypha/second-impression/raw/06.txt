%%TITLE Roadside Pantheon (Exit 47)

== I. Mr. Varga's Palace of Wonders

Itook the job because my van needed a new alternator and Old Varga paid in crisp twenties that smelled faintly of cedar chests and wet tobacco.

His place sat half a mile off Exit 47 on a two-lane stretch of Driftless highway between Dodgeville and Spring Green: a low, windowless sprawl of corrugated tin and faux-fieldstone bolted onto the side of a sandstone ridge, topped by a twenty-foot neon sign that read *VARGA'S PALACE OF WONDERS — CURIOS, AUTOMATA & MYSTERIES OF THE OLD WORLD*. Half the tubes were dead. At dusk it read *VARGA'S PALACE OF ONDERS*, buzzing like a hornet trapped in a mason jar.

I've repaired neon across southern Wisconsin for twenty years. You learn not to ask too many questions about the people who run roadside attractions. Still, Varga was a piece of work—seventy or two hundred, hard to tell under a greasy houndstooth jacket, with bright, crow-black eyes and a Carpathian accent that thickened whenever he was trying to talk me down on parts.

"It's not the transformers, Ray," he said as he led me past the ticket booth into the main labyrinth. His fingers were twitching against the brass coins in his trouser pocket. "The voltage is fine. The meter's spinning like a top. Something's _drinking_ the draw before it hits the glass."

Inside, the museum was bigger than the tin shell outside had any business holding—a cramped, carpeted maze of dim corridors packed floor to ceiling with coin-operated fortune tellers, glass cases of two-headed calves, mechanical violins behind dusty velvet ropes, and dozens of plaster saints whose paint had flaked down to the chalk. Every fifty feet, a tarnished quarter-slot begged for attention: _See the Mechanical Turk! Hear the Singing Canary of Prague! Drop 25¢ for Your True Destiny!_

"Place looks dead, Varga," I said, slinging my tool bag over my shoulder. "Nobody stops off Highway 18 in November."

"They don't need to stop in November," Varga snapped, glancing nervously down a side corridor. "A highway remembers who drove it in July. A quarter dropped in 1978 still pays interest if the wiring's clean. That's how a shrine works, Ray—you give people a wonder, they give you a dime of their attention, and you stay fed. Simple business."

I stopped beside a glass case housing a wax fortune-teller in faded crimson silk. Her jaw was hanging slightly open, and the small red pilot light inside her cabinet was flickering down to a dull, sickly amber.

"You're talking about yourself," I said slowly.

Varga didn't bother denying it. He looked too tired to run the usual hustle. "I came over in a steamer trunk in 1904 with a Slovak glassblower who still knew three of my proper names," he muttered, rubbing his temples. "Back in the Danube hills I had crossroads, blackened spoons, a whole village leaving salt on the sill before frost. Here? Here you adapt or you starve. You build a tourist trap. You put a quarter slot on the altar and call it a museum. It worked for sixty years."

"Until?"

"Until last month," Varga whispered. He pointed a crooked thumb straight down at the stained green indoor-outdoor carpet. "Until whatever's _under the slab_ woke up and started undercutting my rates."

== II. Beneath the Asphalt

I followed the main conduit back through the mechanical-orchestra room, past a twenty-foot ship model built entirely out of varnished toothpicks and a glass diorama of an impossible medieval cathedral, until we reached a padlocked plywood door marked *HALL OF MIRRORS — CLOSED FOR REPAIRS*.

"Don't go in there without checking the load first," Varga warned, hanging back ten feet from the threshold. Even a half-starved old-world trickster knows when a room belongs to somebody bigger.

I put my inductive clamp meter on the heavy armored cable running through the cinderblock wall. The needle slammed past forty amps—enough draw to light a football field—yet the Hall of Mirrors behind the door was dead dark.

And the air leaking under the plywood smelled wrong. It didn't smell like burnt insulation or dusty carpet. It smelled like wet limestone, iron mud, and crushed yellow clover in the middle of November.

"What did you build this parking lot on top of, Varga?" I asked quietly.

He grimaced, spitting once into his handkerchief. "I didn't build it! The guy I bought the lease from in '62 graded the ridge flat for a supper club that went bust. There were some low dirt hills out where the tour-bus turnaround is now—bird-shaped, long tail pointing west. County said they were just old erosion. Dozed 'em flat and poured six inches of concrete over the lot."

I stared at him. Even a guy who wires bar signs knows what a bird-shaped dirt hill on a Driftless ridge is. You don't pour a parking lot over an effigy mound and expect the limestone to forget who held the deed before the concrete truck arrived.

"You're an immigrant, Varga," I said, echoing something my grandfather used to say about the old Cornish and Norwegian families around Mineral Point. "Your whole museum runs on imported stories. And you bolted it straight onto the one patch of rock the glaciers went around."

"I didn't ask for trouble from the dirt!" Varga hissed. "The dirt doesn't even _want_ quarters, Ray! That's what's killing me. If it were a rival god, I could bargain. I could cut a deal, share the gate. This thing isn't taking offerings. It's letting _someone else_ set up a booth inside my back wing."

I unclipped my heavy screwdriver and popped the rusted hasp off the plywood door.

== III. The Rival Booth

The Hall of Mirrors was not forty feet long.

When I swung the door open and shone my Maglite inside, the reflections didn't bounce back the way cheap silvered glass is supposed to. Instead of a cramped zigzag of dusty mirrors, the corridor stretched out into a wide, low-ceilinged promenade paved with damp grey dolomite, lined on both sides by tall, tarnished pier-glasses that didn't show my reflection at all.

In the glass, there was no fluorescent museum, no Exit 47, no November sleet. There was a dark, windless lake under a sky the color of old sulfur, and the broken silhouettes of towers that leaned slightly inward as if listening.

Halfway down the promenade stood a single coin-operated booth I had never seen in any carnival catalogue.

It was built of blackened oak and tarnished brass, draped in tattered lengths of pale ochre velvet. There was no glass front—only an open velvet curtain—and no coin slot at all. Above the lintel, a single hand-bent neon tube burned in a steady, silent, buttery yellow. Not a flicker. Not a sixty-cycle hum. It wasn't even wired to Varga's breaker box; the bare copper leads simply hung loose in the air and drank straight from the room.

Inside the booth sat a tall, narrow figure in heavy yellow-draped linen. Its hands rested calmly on a small wooden table holding two brass tokens and a folded paper program. Where its face should have been, a smooth pale stillness waited—neither mask nor flesh, just a quiet vacancy that made my chest feel suddenly hollow and light, the way you feel at 2:00 AM on a long drive when you forget which town you just passed through.

Behind me, Varga let out a low, terrified whimper from the doorway.

"You see?" he breathed. "No quarter slot. No miracle. No blessing. He doesn't even ask you to believe in him."

The figure in the booth tilted its head slightly toward me. It did not speak aloud, yet the dry, polite cadence of its offer settled straight into the back of my teeth, warm as summer dust:

_Why pay the old hustler for cheap wonders? Why carry the weight of a century that never asked for you? Sit down. Take a program. The first act is over, the road outside is cold, and in here nobody will ever ask you what year you drove in from._

For three seconds, I almost walked down the stone floor and took the chair.

I was fifty-one years old. My knees ached when the barometer dropped, my van was dying in the gravel lot, and half the neon signs I'd bent in my twenties were sitting in landfills behind strip malls. The idea of sitting down in a warm, yellow-lit room where time stopped ticking and nobody asked for a receipt felt sweeter than anything I'd wanted in ten years.

Then my boot heel shifted on the damp dolomite, and I felt a faint, deep vibration coming up through the rubber sole—not from the yellow booth, and not from Varga's dying neon.

It came from _under_ the slab. Slow, immense, and entirely indifferent to both of them. The old ridge itself, unglaciated and heavy, rolling slightly in its sleep beneath six inches of cracked 1962 concrete—neither worshipping the immigrant trickster nor buying a ticket to the pale stranger's play, merely reminding both of them that the stone had been here before the Danube had a name and before Carcosa had a stage.

I didn't smash the yellow neon tube. I had a feeling breaking that glass would spill something onto the carpet neither of us could mop up.

Instead, I stepped back across the threshold, pulled the heavy armored conduit clean out of the cinderblock junction box with my insulated linesman's pliers, and severed the feed. Sparks spat blue against the wet concrete.

== IV. Closing Time

Varga was leaning against the wax fortune-teller's cabinet, panting, his crow-black eyes wide and wet.

"You sealed it," he gasped.

"I bought you a winter," I said, dropping my pliers back into my tool bag. "The booth's still

in there. It doesn't run on your breaker box, Varga. It's running on the hole your landlord dug in that ridge sixty years ago."

He wiped his forehead with the back of his sleeve. Slowly, the old hustler's bravado crept back into his shoulders, though his hands were still shaking. "So what do I do? Rip up the parking lot? Give the dirt back to the birds?"

"You could start by paying me for the service call," I said.

He reached into his houndstooth jacket and counted out six twenties—slightly damp, smelling faintly of tobacco and old copper—and pressed them into my palm. As I walked back out through the maze of mechanical violins and plaster saints, the red pilot light in the fortune-teller's cabinet flickered back to full strength.

Outside, the sleet had turned to freezing rain. I stood by my van for a minute, looking up at the dark tin roof of the Palace of Wonders and the shadowed crest of the bluff rising behind it into the fog.

High on the roof, the repaired neon sign buzzed cleanly against the November dark— every letter lit except one. The *W* in _WONDERS_ had gone dead again, leaving a narrow yellow reflection in the wet blacktop of the parking lot, shaped unmistakably like the spread wings of a bird pointing west.

I got into the van, turned the key twice until the alternator caught, and drove back toward the highway without looking in the rearview mirror.

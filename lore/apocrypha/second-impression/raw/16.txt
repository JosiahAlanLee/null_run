%%TITLE The Man Who Kept the Ledger for the Glaciers

Every February, on the first clear morning after the river froze, old Martin Halvorsen walked the four-mile perimeter of Section 16 with a brass pocket compass, a birch walking-stick, and a county plat book bound in cracked calfskin.

He had been a land agent for forty years before he retired, and he knew the geology of the county the way a watchmaker knows the teeth of an escapement. To the east, the glaciers had ground the hills into neat gravel plains and polite cornfields. To the north, they had left twenty feet of scraped cobble and boulder clay. Only here, across the steep dolomite ridges and lead-veined hollows of Section 16, had the great ice sheets parted like water around a boulder and flowed around without touching the crest.

_Drift: nil,_ the old 1872 ironworks boring logs read on every single hole from six chains west to fifty-two.

Martin did not believe in ghosts, and he had little patience for the tourist pamphlets that tried to turn the valley's old stories into charming folklore. What he believed in was _seizin_—the older, heavier law that a piece of ground remembers everyone who ever stood on it, and keeps its own books long after the courthouse burns.

He stopped at the southwest corner post of Section 16, a lichen-crusted block of dressed dolomite set into the ridge in October 1891. Twenty-five paces before he reached the stone, the red needle of his pocket compass drifted lazily off north and refused to settle. He slipped the compass back into his vest and laid his bare palm against the north face of the limestone outcrop beside the post.

The air stood at sixteen degrees. The sun had not touched that north face since autumn. Under his palm, the dolomite was steadily, unmistakably warm.

West of the ruined ironworks foundation, low earth-banks ran along the lip of the ridge in the long, quiet curve of spread wings pointing toward the river. The county survey of 1872 had entered them as _natural wash_; the ironworks foreman had written _We do not cut there_ in the margin of his boring log and left his shovels on the east side of the line.

Martin stood quietly beside the stone post and let the February stillness settle. In unglaciated country, when the wind drops and the frost holds the oak leaves flat, time does not feel like a river running past you. It feels like a solid block of layered limestone —1872, 1891, 1953, and this cold morning all sitting side by side in the same bedding plane, separated by nothing thicker than a sheet of tracing vellum.

Halfway down the hollow, where the old dry stone sink sat near the center of the section, he could almost hear the faint metallic clink of a surveyor's chain being dragged across frozen turf—eighty chains east, eighty chains south, seventy-seven twelve west, seventy-seven eighty-four north, always falling three point six zero chains short, always pulling inward toward the empty center where the fifth iron pin had been driven. Once, three winters ago, he had seen a thin man in a grey 1950s wool coat standing near the ironworks foundation with a tin dispatch box under his arm, checking a barometer that never moved from 989 millibars; when Martin blinked against the snow-glare, there was only a red cedar and a drift of dry oak leaves.

Beneath his boots, deep down in the lead-veined rock, three slow, unhurried knocks sounded—_knock, knock, knock_—spaced evenly, as if someone in an unmapped adit far below the ridge were testing the timbering.

Martin did not hurry away. He reached into his mackinaw pocket, broke off the dry heel of the rye roll he had brought for his walk, and set it neatly on the flat top of the southwest stone post. A courtesy to older tenants costs nothing, and the ground has a long memory for manners.

Then he walked north along the ridge to the fourth corner post, checked the frosted turf at its base—just to be certain no coiled steel chain had been left there since last winter—and turned back down the gravel lane toward town, leaving Section 16 to keep its own balance in the dark.

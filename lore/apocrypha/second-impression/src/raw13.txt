%%TITLE The Unfinished Room at the End of the Corridor

Before they condemned the east wing of the St. Jude Sanatorium, the historical society hired me to photograph every corridor on medium-format film.

It was honest, cold work: peeling institutional plaster, quarry-red herringbone tile laid in 1924, heavy oak doors swollen shut by forty winters of river damp. I worked alone from seven until noon, setting my tripod on the exact centerline of each hall so the vanishing points would stay clean.

I noticed the error in the darkroom on Thursday night.

I had photographed the third-floor east corridor from the stairwell doorway, looking straight down toward a blank cinderblock bulkhead two hundred and sixteen feet away. On the contact sheet, however, the corridor did not end at the bulkhead. Six feet past where the wall should have stood, the red herringbone tile continued cleanly under an open arch into a low, dim chamber lit by a single amber bulkhead lamp.

I blamed a double exposure—until I went back on Friday morning with a steel tape and a pair of work gloves.

The third-floor corridor was empty, smelling of floor wax and wet lime. Yet halfway down the hall, two pale masonry scars ran straight across the herringbone floor where partition walls had been struck and their sills grubbed out. Beyond the second scar, the plaster ended abruptly.

On the left wall stood a wide, riveted steel window frame pressed flush against deep, motionless green water—green all the way to the sill, with a thin ridge of pale silt settled along the bottom edge of the glass. Six feet further down the same wall, a bank of iron ventilation louvres was sealed across its third row with crisp white rime, breathing a dry chill that smelled of high polar ice. And beyond the louvres, the corridor wall ceased to be masonry at all, opening onto raw, tool-marked limestone studded with tiny spiral marine fossils.

Three incompatible worlds bolted onto one continuous hallway, and not a single seam leaked.

At the far end of the corridor, beneath a steady amber bulkhead lamp whose two braided cloth leads hung completely loose and unconnected in the air, a slight man in a damp wool coat sat on a folding wooden stool. A brass stopwatch lay open in his palm.

"You're six feet early," he said politely, without looking up from the dial. "The demolition crew isn't scheduled until Monday."

"There's a lake behind that glass," I said. My voice came back to me half a second late, as if the hall were three times wider than its walls.

"So the northern wing claims," he murmured, making a neat pencil mark in a pocket ledger. "Though the louvre beside it disagrees by forty degrees, and the limestone past the lamp denies there is an outside at all. Architects are terribly quarrelsome once they stop being paid."

I lifted my Hasselblad without thinking, framing him against the unplugged lamp and the three-climate wall. Through the ground-glass viewfinder, the corridor looked narrower than it had a moment ago—the walls drawing smoothly inward by a few inches, the floor tiles shortening their perspective with the quiet, effortless finality of a stage flat being pulled back into the wings.

"I wouldn't take that frame," he said, glancing up at me at last. His eyes were tired, dry, and entirely sane. "Every time someone measures this hall, it loses another six feet. Keep photographing it and there won't be enough corridor left to walk back to the stairs."

I lowered the camera. When I turned to check the exit behind me, the stairwell door was already closer than it had been when I walked in—and the green exit transom above it burned steady, every letter lit except the *R*, which had faded cleanly to dark glass.

I backed out of the corridor without taking the picture, packed my tripod in the stairwell, and turned in my contact sheets to the historical society on Saturday morning. They tore down the east wing the following Tuesday. I drove past the rubble lot in the rain on Wednesday afternoon; the red herringbone tiles were gone, the brick was stacked for salvage, and standing completely untouched in the center of the muddy foundation slab was a single square of dry limestone, warm to the touch, with no tracks leading away from it.

%%TITLE The Clerk of Unwritten Drafts

== I. Aisle 409 (Unfinished — Temperance & Melancholy)

The trouble with mortals is that they think an archive is a place where dead things are kept quiet. They have never worked the night shift in the Sleeping Stacks.

My name is Simeon, and for four hundred and twelve years—give or take a decade during the Napoleonic Wars, when the clocks in the East Wing melted into tallow—I have been an Assistant Shelver in the Department of Unwritten Drafts. Our jurisdiction is simple: every novel a mortal abandons on page nineteen, every letter burned in shame over a kitchen sink, every epic poem composed in the shower and forgotten before the towel is dry eventually drifts down through the ceiling vents and settles onto our returns carts.

We catalogue them, bind them in soft grey buckram, and shelve them alongside the waking world's real editions so the floorboards stay level. It is humid, quiet work. The air smells of damp cedar, iron-gall ink, and the faint ozone of half-formed sentences. I am fond of the Stacks. There is a tenderness to a book nobody ever had the courage to finish; it does not shout, it does not demand royalties, and it never tries to climb off the shelf.

Until Tuesday, when the yellow pamphlet landed on Cart 14.

I knew it was illegal the moment I picked it up. An unwritten book is light as balsa wood —all intention and no ballast. This slim, paper-wrapped octavo weighed nearly eleven pounds. It sagged my wrist toward the floorboards, and the ochre paper felt warm and slightly greasy, like an old theatre program kept too close to a radiator.

There was no author's name on the spine. No date. Only a title stamped in faded gilt that refused to stay in the same typeface when I blinked: a two-act play set in a city beside a dark lake, featuring a masked ball and a king who never appears onstage until the curtain has already been drawn.

I checked the intake ledger. No entry. I checked the Burned-in-Shame annex. Nothing.

Then I checked the *Citation Index*, and my stomach dropped.

The book had never been written _or_ unwritten. It had simply been _quoted_—thousands of times, across a century of waking-world short stories, occult pamphlets, late-night forum posts, and footnotes in books that didn't exist either—by people who had never read a single page of it. All that borrowed belief had condensed in the damp air of the Stacks like iron filings around a magnet, precipitating a physical copy out of pure hearsay.

By the time I reached the Chief Archivist's desk to file a Quarantine Writ, the pamphlet had already begun to leak.

Yellow ink was seeping through the grey buckram of the neighboring volumes on Cart 14. An abandoned 1954 romance from Duluth had lost its third chapter; in its place, neat italicized stage directions instructed the heroine to lay aside her coat and listen for the seventh beat of the organ. A half-written parish history from Sauk County had sprouted a cast list on its flyleaf. Worse, the cedar floorboards beneath the cart were softening into wet limestone, and the warm, sweet smell of out-of-season alder pollen was drifting down Aisle 409.

"You cannot incinerate a hearsay text," the Chief Archivist said, not looking up from his magnifying glass. He was a thin, soot-colored figure who had been made out of the margin-notes of three lost Alexandrian rolls, and he hated paperwork even more than contagion. "Burn it here, and the smoke goes straight back up the vents into forty thousand bedrooms. You have to go _out_."

"Out?" I repeated. I had not worn shoes on waking-world pavement since 1888, and even then it had given me a migraine for a fortnight.

"Someone up there is acting as the press," the Chief said, tapping a brass barometer whose needle was spinning slowly counterclockwise. "An insomniac. Someone reading fragments in the dark and dreaming the missing second act hard enough to give it weight on our shelves. Find them. Close the loop. And Simeon—take a coat. It is November in Wisconsin."

== II. The Rain-Door (3:14 AM, U.S. Highway 12)

Crossing out is always uglier than mortals imagine. There is no wardrobe, no shimmering pool of starlight. You open the utility closet behind the Periodicals desk, step over a mop bucket full of grey water, and push through a heavy steel fire door that smells of diesel exhaust and stale coffee.

I came out behind a vending machine in a Greyhound bus depot on the edge of a wet Wisconsin town at three-fourteen in the morning.

The waking world always shocks me with how _thin_ it is—how flat the light looks under fluorescent tubes, how little room there is behind the linoleum. Outside the glass doors, November rain fell straight down across an empty asphalt lot, turning the red neon of a closed motel sign into smeared grease on the puddles.

Only one person was waiting in the depot.

She sat on a bolted plastic bench near the ticket window, a woman in her late thirties wearing faded hospital scrubs under a damp fleece jacket. Beside her sat a lukewarm paper cup of coffee and a cheap college-ruled spiral notebook. Her eyes were red-rimmed with the brittle, luminous exhaustion of someone who had been awake for forty hours, and her ballpoint pen was moving across the lined paper in a tight, rhythmic slant that never paused at the margins.

Every time her pen reached the bottom of a page, the fluorescent tube directly overhead buzzed a half-tone flatter, and a faint smear of yellow dust drifted out of the heating grate by her boots.

I smoothed my grey wool coat—borrowed from the Lost Property rack in the Stacks— and sat down two seats away.

"You're writing the wrong half," I said quietly.

She didn't jump. People who have been awake for forty hours lose the border between surprise and weather. She merely glanced sideways at me, her pen hovering half an inch above the paper.

"Excuse me?"

"Act Two," I said, nodding toward her notebook. "The part where the masks come off. You think you're just transcribing something you half-remembered from an old paperback you read in college. You aren't. You're typesetting it. And it's ruining three hundred yards of perfectly good shelving downstairs."

She stared at me for a long moment. In the harsh depot light, I could see how tired she was—not merely sleepy, but worn thin in the way people get when they spend twelve hours a night typing up other people's diagnoses and go home to an apartment that is too quiet to hold them.

"Maren," she said, almost to herself, testing whether I was real. "Wait—who are you?"

"Simeon. Assistant Shelver, Department of Unwritten Drafts." I reached into my coat pocket and laid a dry, folded index card on the plastic seat between us. It was stamped in pale violet ink: _RECALL NOTICE — UNCATALOGUED CONTAGION_. "You don't have to believe I'm real, Maren. Just look at your fingers."

She looked down. Packed beneath the nail of her right index finger, where she gripped the cheap Bic pen, was a fine, bright crescent of damp yellow pollen.

Her breath hitched. She closed the spiral notebook slowly, pressing her palm flat against the cardboard cover as if holding down a lid.

"I couldn't sleep," she whispered. "Every time I closed my eyes after a double shift, I kept hearing this—this cadence. Like a play someone was rehearsing in the next apartment, except the next apartment is empty. I thought if I wrote it down, it would get out of my head."

"That works for ordinary ghosts," I said gently. "Not for this one. This one _wants_ to be written down. Every line you put on paper gives it another pound of weight in the Stacks. Give it another twenty minutes, and the floor under Aisle 409 gives way—and then it isn't just in your notebook. It's in the depot."

As if to underline my point, the glass doors rattled once, though the rain outside was falling dead vertical with no wind. Out by the bus bay, in the amber wash of the sodium lamp, a tall figure in a wet, ochre-colored raincoat stood motionless with its back to the street, facing the depot glass. It had no luggage.

Maren saw it. Her knuckles went white on the spiral wire.

"How do I stop it?" she asked, her voice shaking. "Burn the notebook?"

"No," I said. "Burning only sends it down to my department. You have to trade it."

== III. The Exchange Rate

This is the part of the job I have never liked. A mortal can cross into a dream for free, if they are careless enough; closing a leak from the waking side always requires a tariff, because the Stacks obey conservation of weight. If I take eleven pounds of stolen hearsay off her hands, I have to leave something behind—or take something equally solid in its place so the ledger balances.

"I need a memory you haven't lost yet," I told her. "Not a bad one. Bad memories are light; everybody tries to unload them. Something small and specific that you still use to orient yourself. The layout of a house you grew up in. The sound of a particular voice. The taste of a winter morning before you knew what grief was."

Maren looked out at the figure in the yellow raincoat. It had taken one slow step closer to the glass. Even through the closed doors, I could smell wet dolomite and bruised quince over the depot's bleach.

"If I give it to you," she asked, "what happens to the play?"

"I file it under _Non-Existent, Verified_," I said. "I bind it in lead-lined buckram and put it on the bottom shelf beside three unwritten treatises by Giordano Bruno and a love letter Franz Kafka never posted. It stays quiet."

"And what happens to me?"

"You go home," I said. "You sleep for twelve hours. And tomorrow morning, there will be one small room in your head where the furniture has been moved out, and you won't quite be able to remember what used to sit by the window."

She looked down at the spiral notebook for five seconds. Then she unscrewed the cap of her pen, tore the filled pages cleanly out of the wire coil, and held them out to me.

"Take the summer I was nine," she said steadily. "My grandfather's screened porch in Baraboo. The way the cedar boards smelled after a storm, and the exact creak the third step made when I snuck out before dawn to watch the mist come off the river. Keep it somewhere dry."

I took the torn pages. Instantly, they grew heavy in my palm—eleven pounds of damp, yellow-edged hearsay collapsing back into quiet ink. At the same moment, a warm, sharp scent of wet cedar and river-mist slid out of her eyes and into my coat pocket, folding itself neatly into a slim, unbound chapbook that weighed almost nothing at all.

Outside, the sodium lamp flickered once. When it steadied, the bus bay was empty. Only the rain fell across the black asphalt.

Maren blinked, rubbed her eyes with the back of her hand, and let out a long, ragged yawn—the first honest sleepiness she had felt in two days. She looked at the empty spiral notebook on her lap with mild confusion, as if wondering why she had brought it to a bus depot.

"Did I miss the 3:30 to Madison?" she murmured.

"It's running five minutes late," I said, standing up and buttoning my grey coat. "You have plenty of time."

I walked back around the vending machine, pushed open the heavy steel utility door, and stepped back into the humid cedar dark of the Sleeping Stacks.

I locked the torn pages of Act II in the lead-lined cabinet at the end of Aisle 409, signed the Quarantine Writ, and checked the floorboards. They were solid wood again.

Before I went off shift, I walked down to the quietest corner of the East Wing—the shelf where we keep things that were never meant to be published—and bound Maren's cedar porch in clean blue linen. I did not stamp a title on the spine. I simply shelved it where the air stays dry, between an unmailed apology from 1919 and a lullaby nobody has sung since the last glacier melted, and turned down the lamp.

== I. Note on Provenance

I am placing this statement in Box 14-C not because I expect the Chancellor to read it, nor because I believe it belongs among the baptismal registers of the lower Wisconsin River valley, but because an archivist's only remaining defense against an uncatalogued fact is to give it a shelf mark.

For twenty-two years I have kept the parish records of St. Jude's in order. I prefer things that stay inside their margins: vestry minutes, cemetery plats, coal receipts from 1911, the neat copperplate of dead rectors who believed the world was a well-lit room overseen by a Landlord who kept his own books. When I was twenty-three and still at the seminary in Nashotah, I thought the same. I read the old Inkling novels and the Argentine labyrinths and supposed that the uncanny came in only two temperatures: the cool geometry of a library with no exit, or the bright, supervised revel of a wood where the Lion stands just behind the birches to make sure the Maenads do not bite through the skin.

I no longer hold either view. There are things in the Driftless that predate the parish, and there are texts that do not keep a chaperone.

The pamphlet came to us in the autumn of 2004 as part of the estate of Father Edmund Hale, a retired priest who had lived alone in a limestone cottage above the ferry landing at Merrimac. Hale had spent six years in London between 1894 and 1900 as a curate in Bloomsbury, at a time when half the literary clergy of that city were slipping out of Evensong to sit in rented rooms off Chancery Lane with Rosicrucians and failed symbolists. Most of his bequest was harmless: bound volumes of _The Hibbert Journal_, a worn copy of Waite's _Hidden Church of the Holy Graal_, two shelves of patristics smelling of pipe ash and cellar mold.

Tucked inside the hollow back of a folio _Concordance_—not hidden with any theatrical cunning, merely shelved where a tired man puts a book he does not wish to burn—was an octavo pamphlet sewn in faded ochre wrappers. Its title page read:

> _PROLEGOMENA TO THE SECOND MASQUE:_

> _Being Fragments of an Office for the Uncrowned Season, with Rubric and Responses Done into English from the French of an Unnamed Hand_

> _London: Privately Printed at the Sign of the Pale Moth, 1898_

There were thirty-six pages. The first fourteen were liturgical apparatus of a kind I recognized from Hale's other London curiosities: instructions for posture, breath, the positioning of three readers against a natural stone reflector, and the timing of the reading at civil twilight in late October, "when the sap hath gone down into the root and the leaf is no longer honest."

The remaining twenty-two pages were dramatic dialogue, divided into two movements. The first movement was almost embarrassingly polite. Three courtiers—a Steward, a Chorister, and a Stranger who is never given a proper name—wait in a damp pavilion beside a still lake for a host who has sent word that the supper will begin once the masks are laid aside. Their speeches were stiff, ornate, a little faded, like late-Victorian verse drama translated by someone who had read too much Maeterlinck. There was a short song about twin suns sinking behind a grey ridge and a city whose towers cast no shadow on the water.

The second movement was different. At the top of page nineteen, the verse broke off. The speech prefixes disappeared, replaced by narrow columns of Latinate stage directions and physiological rubrics. They did not tell an actor how to stand or project his voice. They described the dilation of the throat, the taste of copper under the tongue, the softening of the cartilage behind the ear, and the exact interval—counted in heartbeats, not bars—at which the body ceases to belong to the calendar and reverts to the ground it is standing on.

I should have logged it under _Curiosa, Unverified_ and locked it in the fireproof cabinet. Instead, I carried it back to the river house I was sharing with Martin Kowalski and Clare Lindholm.

== II. The Three Readers

It is important to be exact about who we were, because the pamphlet did not work on us as an idea. It worked on what we already wanted.

Martin was the sharpest of us and the least suited to orders. He had come to theology through the back door of Renaissance hermeticism and 1920s weird fiction; he talked about cosmology the way a machinist talks about an engine someone had assembled in the dark and then lied about in the manual. He did not believe the universe was indifferent, and he did not believe it was providential. He believed it was _authored_—and badly edited—by someone who had cut whole chapters out to make the rest look tidy. When I showed him the 1898 pamphlet, he didn't laugh at the Pale Moth imprint. He held the ochre paper up to the kitchen bulb to check the watermark, then ran his thumb over the stitched spine.

"It's not a hoax," he said. "Hoaxes try to sound ancient. This reads like a repair manual."

Clare was different. She was two years older than us, an organist and choir-school accompanist who audited systematic theology because she liked the arguments about substance and accident. She had a quiet, physical certainty that made both Martin and me seem like paper cutouts. Where Martin wanted to know how the door was hinged and I wanted to know who held the key, Clare only cared about how a room sounded when you sang into it.

She read the first movement aloud at the kitchen table while the kettle boiled, tapping the rhythm against her collarbone with two fingers. When she reached page nineteen, she did not stop. Her voice dropped half a register, and the small yellow light over the sink seemed, for a second, to sit closer to her skin than to the plaster walls.

"You can't read this in a wooden room," she said, closing the pamphlet over her thumb. "The rubric says limestone. Damp stone, open at the top, with water below it."

"There are fifty coulees like that between here and Lodi," Martin said.

"I know one," she answered.

I told myself I went along to keep them from making fools of themselves. That is the polite version. The truer version is that I had spent three years reading about miracles that always happened in someone else's century, in someone else's country, and I was sick to death of footnotes. I thought—God forgive the arrogance of a twenty-three-year-old seminarian—that if there really was a wilder country folded underneath the county road, it would still be a _safe_ wildness. I actually remember quoting _Prince Caspian_ to Clare as we walked up the gravel track in the dark: the part where Susan says she wouldn't have felt safe with Bacchus and the wild girls if Aslan hadn't been there.

Clare didn't look back at me. She was carrying her choir notebook and a brass lantern she had borrowed from the vestry.

"Who told you he's here?" she asked.

== IV. What Happened in the Hollow

I have tried, over the years, to write down what occurred between 5:50 and 6:05 without using words that belong to pulp magazines or sermons. Neither vocabulary fits.

When Clare finished the song of the twin suns, the hollow did not open onto a fantasy landscape. There were no marble towers, no black stars hanging over Sauk County. That would have been easier to bear. What happened instead was that the _dimensions of the dusk_ went wrong.

The limestone walls around us were forty feet apart when we walked in. By the time Martin began the Stranger's fourth speech, I could no longer see the opposite ledge, though the lantern was burning brighter than before—a thick, buttery yellow that pooled on the wet dolomite like oil. The air grew warm. Not summer-warm; it was the close, animal heat of a crowded room where too many people have been breathing for hours without opening a window.

And then the sound arrived.

It was not a voice speaking words. It was a low, brassy resonance—like an organ pipe drawn on a sixteen-foot stop, played softly enough that you feel it in the sternum before you hear it in the ear—and inside that vibration, something stepped down from the upper rim of the bluff.

I want to be precise: I did not see a monster. I saw a tall, narrow figure in a heavy garment the color of dead willow leaves and tarnished brass, moving down the wet talus with the unhurried certainty of a host coming out to greet guests who have arrived late to his table. Where a face should have been, there was a pale, smooth stillness that my eyes refused to resolve—not blank, not featureless, simply _unfixed_, as if the flesh were waiting to see what appetite we had brought with us before deciding what mouth to wear.

There was no Lion behind the birches. There was no elder guardian, no moral counterweight, no neat division between the bright court and the dark one. There was only _hunger_—vast, courtly, intimate, and entirely unembarrassed.

I felt it in my own body first, and that is what broke me. My teeth ached in their sockets as if I were twelve years old again and growing too fast for my jaw. A fierce, humiliating warmth flooded my chest and throat, the exact physical sensation of starving after a long fast and suddenly smelling roast meat and wine—except that what I wanted was not food. I wanted to take off my coat, kneel in the wet iron-mud, and let the figure in the willow-colored linen tell me what my name was for the second half of the evening. Five hours seemed to pass in the space of a single breath. When I looked down at my hands, a fine yellow pollen—thick, slightly damp, smelling of alder and crushed marigold—was packed beneath the moons of my fingernails.

Martin was already on his knees on the upper bench, laughing quietly—not the laugh of a man losing his mind, which is thin and brittle, but the deep, relieved laugh of someone who has been searching for a door his whole life and has finally found one that opens inward. Clare had set the lantern on the stone. Her eyes were wide and bright, her mouth slightly open, her fingers moving against her thigh as if she were already playing the accompaniment to something none of us had heard yet.

"Arthur," she said, and her voice sounded as if it were coming from the far end of a long, stone corridor. "Your line."

I looked at the page in my hand. The printed lines had not changed, and yet I knew— with a sudden, sickening certainty—that if I spoke the next three words, I would not be standing in Sauk County anymore, or rather that Sauk County would no longer be the thing standing underneath my boots.

I dropped the pamphlet. I grabbed Clare's notebook by mistake as I lunged for the lantern, tore two pages clean off the wire coil, and scrambled backward up the wet scree on my hands and knees.

Nobody chased me. That was the worst part. As I clawed my way over the lip of the bluff, tearing my palms on the juniper roots, I looked back once. The lantern was still burning on the flat rock, casting a huge, motionless circle of yellow light. Martin and Clare were standing close together, facing the figure at the center of the hollow. The figure had raised one gloved hand—not in threat, not in blessing, simply the gesture a conductor makes a beat before the downstroke.

I ran three miles down County C in the dark. At the Citgo outside Prairie du Sac, I locked myself in the restroom and scrubbed my hands with pink grit soap until the skin over my knuckles split. The yellow dust came off the skin, mostly. Under the thumbnails, it stayed for six days, until the nails grew out.

== V. The Twenty-Two-Year Intermission

A man can build a very sturdy cage out of routine if he starts early enough.

I withdrew from the seminary that December, citing "nervous exhaustion," and took a lay position cataloging diocesan records. By 2008 I was permanent archivist at St. Jude's. I stopped reading fiction. I stopped walking in the bluffs after four in the afternoon. I bought a small frame house on the flat alluvial plain where the glaciers had at least had the decency to leave gravel behind, and I told myself a tidy story about what had happened to Martin and Clare.

I told myself they had suffered a shared psychotic break brought on by stress, isolation, and bad Victorian occultism.

The external facts almost cooperated. Martin dropped out of seminary in January 2005. For two months he sent me long, cramped letters from a rented room in Baraboo—no return address, postmarked from different river towns—filled with diagrams of stage blocking and marginal notes about "the exchange rate of the second act." I burned them unread after the fourth one. In March, his landlady told the police he had paid his rent three months ahead in cash and walked out leaving his winter coat on the peg. He was never declared dead, only missing.

Clare stayed longer. She resigned from the choir school in the spring of 2005 and took an apprenticeship at a small hand-bindery in Mineral Point, sewing signatures and tooling leather for private collectors. I saw her once, from across the street in Dodgeville, in the summer of 2011. She was carrying a parcel wrapped in brown kraft paper. She looked straight at me through the traffic, smiled without the slightest trace of anger or madness, and walked on. I told myself she had recovered and moved on with her life.

I should have known better. A liturgy does not end simply because one of the servers walks out of the chancel. It only pauses until the response is supplied.

== VI. Evensong, November 15, 2026

Four days ago was the third Sunday of November. Rain had been falling off and on since morning, the cold, flat drizzle that turns the Wisconsin River the color of old pewter. Because of the weather, only eleven parishioners came to the five o'clock Evensong.

I sat in the archivist's stall near the north transept, as I always do, where I can check the humidity gauge on the oak paneling. Our regular organist, Mrs. Geller, had called in with bronchitis; the rector had announced before the service that a substitute from Iowa County had generously offered to play the voluntary and the hymns.

I did not look up toward the loft during the prelude. I was thinking about a leak in the roof above the 1930s marriage registers.

Then the postlude began.

It was not in the hymnal. It was a slow, processional cadence in an uneven meter— seven beats where the ear expected six—played on the softest reed stops, with a sixteen-foot pedal note underneath that made the oak rail of my stall hum against my palms. The church air, which had smelled all afternoon of damp wool and floor wax, shifted. For three seconds I smelled bruised quince, wet dolomite, and alder pollen in the middle of November.

My jaw began to ache.

I stood up so fast my prayer book slid off the ledge and hit the flagstones. The rector was already at the west door shaking hands with the last two parishioners. Up in the organ loft, the lamp over the music desk clicked off.

I did not go up the stairs. I walked down the side aisle toward the narthex, and halfway there I stopped.

Clare was standing by the last pew.

She was forty-seven years old, or should have been. She did not look young in the way people mean when they talk about fairy tales—there was nothing glassy or preserved about her. She looked _uninterrupted_. Her coat was dry despite the rain outside. Her skin had the flushed, high-colored warmth of someone who had just come in from a crowded ballroom, and when she looked at me, I saw that the thing in the hollow had never broken her mind. It had simply given her work to do.

"Hello, Arthur," she said. Her voice was low and steady, carrying down the empty nave without an echo. "You kept the dry pages."

"Martin?" I managed to say.

"He's already on," she said, glancing once toward the dark chancel. "He sends his regards. He says the acoustics are much better from the other side of the curtain."

She did not come any closer. She did not threaten me or offer me a hand. She simply reached into the pocket of her dry wool coat, set Hymnal \#114 gently on the end of the back pew, and smoothed the cloth cover once with her palm. Beneath the rim of her thumbnail, I saw a faint, unmistakable crescent of yellow dust.

"We held the cue as long as we could," she said softly. "You really shouldn't keep a host waiting twenty-two years."

Then she pushed open the heavy oak door and stepped out into the river fog. By the time I reached the porch, the gravel drive was empty, and the rain was falling straight down through the sodium streetlight with no footprint in the wet leaves.

== VII. Exhibit B: Pages Removed from Hymnal \#114

I carried the hymnal back to my desk in the archive room and locked the door.

On the outside it is an ordinary parish copy, stamped _St. Jude-in-the-Bluffs_ on the front paste-down. At the very back, however, just after the index of metrical tunes, two extra leaves have been tipped in and sewn directly into the spine with waxed yellow linen thread. The craftsmanship is immaculate—a Mineral Point hand-bindery stitch, tight enough to outlast the rest of the book.

The paper is heavy, laid rag stock, identical in weight and watermark to Father Hale's 1898 pamphlet. The typeface is the same battered Caslon.

The first leaf is headed:

> *ACT II, SCENE iii*

> _(The Steward's Room. A desk, a locked box, a single lamp. Outside, the river.)_

The stage directions occupy half the page. They describe the exact dimensions of my archive room, down to the brass hygrometer on the north wall and the tin box in my bottom drawer. They note that the Steward has spent twenty-two years trying to turn a doorway into a filing cabinet, and that the attempt has been noted with amusement.

At the top of the second leaf, there is a single line of dialogue assigned to *THE STEWARD*, followed by a blank space where the rubric instructs him to speak his own name aloud and lay aside his coat.

I have read the cue line six times. I am copying it here so that the record in Box 14-C will be complete, even though I know—as I watch the desk lamp lengthen its flame and feel the warm, sweet dust beginning to coat the back of my throat—that copying it is the same as saying it:

> _THE STEWARD: I am placing this statement in Box 14-C not because I expect the Chancellor to read it, nor because I believe it belongs among the baptismal registers of the lower Wisconsin River valley, but because an archivist's only remaining defense against an uncatalogued fact is to give it a shelf mark._

The organ in the dark nave has just begun the seventh beat. I am going to leave the box unlocked.

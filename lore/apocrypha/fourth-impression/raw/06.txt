%%TITLE Roadside Pantheon (Exit 47)

== I. Mr. Varga's Palace of Wonders

Itook the job because my van needed a new alternator and Old Varga paid in crisp twenties that smelled faintly of cedar chests and wet tobacco.

His place sat half a mile off Exit 47 on a two-lane stretch of Driftless highway between Dodgeville and Spring Green: a low, windowless sprawl of corrugated tin and faux-fieldstone bolted onto the side of a sandstone ridge, topped by a twenty-foot neon sign reading *VARGA'S PALACE OF WONDERS — CURIOS, AUTOMATA & MYSTERIES OF THE OLD WORLD*. Half the tubes were dead. At dusk it read *VARGA'S PALACE OF ONDERS*, buzzing like a hornet trapped in a mason jar.

"It's not the transformers, Ray," Varga said—seventy or two hundred under his greasy houndstooth jacket, crow-black eyes bright, fingers twitching against the brass coins in his pocket. "The voltage is fine. The meter's spinning like a top. Something's _drinking_ the draw before it hits the glass."

Inside, the museum was bigger than the tin shell outside had any business holding—a cramped, carpeted maze of coin-operated fortune tellers, two-headed calves, mechanical violins behind dusty velvet ropes, and plaster saints flaked down to the chalk. "Place looks dead, Varga," I said. "Nobody stops off Highway 18 in November."

"A quarter dropped in July pays in November," Varga snapped, glancing down a side corridor. "A quarter dropped in 1978 still pays interest, provided nobody else puts a meter on it."

Beside us sat a wax fortune-teller in faded crimson silk, her jaw slightly open. The pilot lamp inside her cabinet had faded from red to a dull, dry amber while we stood there. "You're talking about yourself," I said slowly.

Varga looked too tired to make it a pitch. "I came over in a steamer trunk in 1904 with a Slovak glassblower who still knew three of my proper names," he muttered. "Back in the Danube hills I had crossroads, blackened spoons, a whole village leaving salt on the sill before frost. Here? You put the price where people can see it. You put a quarter slot on the altar and call it a museum. It worked for sixty years—until last month, when whatever's under the slab woke up and started undercutting my rates."

== II. Beneath the Asphalt

I followed the main conduit past a twenty-foot toothpick ship model and a glass diorama of an impossible cathedral to a padlocked plywood door marked *HALL OF MIRRORS — CLOSED FOR REPAIRS*. Varga stayed back by the violins. My inductive clamp meter read over forty amps—a hard draw for a room where every lamp was out—and the air leaking under the plywood smelled of wet limestone, iron mud, and crushed yellow clover in November.

"What did you build this parking lot on top of, Varga?" He spat into his handkerchief. "I didn't build it! The guy I bought the lease from in ' graded the ridge flat for a supper club. There were low dirt hills where the tour-bus turnaround is now—bird-shaped, long tail pointing west. County said they were just old erosion. Dozed 'em flat and poured six inches of concrete over the lot."

At the ticket booth he'd framed a copy of the grading plan. The bank was drawn out in a clean, fine line, with one word above it: _WASH_. The blue line that marked the parking lot went straight through the long tail. I unclipped my heavy screwdriver and popped the rusted hasp off the plywood door.

== III. The Rival Booth

The Hall of Mirrors was not forty feet long. Instead of cheap silvered glass, my Maglite opened onto a wide promenade paved with damp grey dolomite, lined by tall, tarnished pier-glasses that showed no reflection of me at all—only a dark, windless lake under a sulfur sky, and broken towers leaning slightly inward as if listening.

Halfway down stood a single booth of blackened oak, tarnished brass, and tattered ochre velvet. Somebody had put a wooden chair where the quarter slot ought to be. Above the lintel a hand-bent yellow neon tube burned steady without a sixty-cycle hum; its two bare copper leads hung free in the air. Inside sat a tall, narrow figure in yellow-draped linen, hands resting calmly beside two brass tokens and a folded paper program, its face a smooth pale stillness.

"You see?" Varga whimpered from the doorway. "No quarter slot. No miracle. He doesn't even ask you to believe in him."

The figure tilted its head a fraction. A dry, polite offer settled behind my teeth: _Sit down. Take a program. You can leave the receipt on the table; in here nobody will ever ask you what year you drove in from._

For three seconds I almost walked down the stone floor and took the chair. I was fifty-one, my knees ached when the barometer dropped, and half the neon signs I'd bent in my twenties were sitting in landfills. Then my boot heel shifted on the damp dolomite, and a deep vibration came up through the rubber sole—not from the booth, and not from Varga's neon.

Three unhurried strokes came up from under the slab—the old unglaciated ridge itself rolling slightly in its sleep beneath six inches of cracked concrete, reminding both the immigrant trickster and #struck who held the stone before either had a stage. By the third stroke I had my heel off the dolomite. I backed across the sill, pulled the armored conduit clean out of the junction box with my insulated linesman's pliers, and severed the feed.

The unplugged yellow tube burned on, but a twenty-foot run of dolomite floor between two consecutive mirrors folded silently out of sight like tidewater pulling off a flat. I slammed the plywood door shut and drove three three-inch deck screws straight through the frame into the stud.

== IV. Closing Time

"I bought you a winter," I told Varga, dropping my pliers into the bag. "There's still a chair in there. It isn't running on your breaker box; it's running on the hole your landlord dug in that ridge."

He counted out six damp twenties smelling of tobacco and old copper. As I passed the wax fortune-teller, her pilot light had worked its way back to red. Outside, freezing rain fell across the blacktop. High on the roof, every repaired letter glowed clean except the *W* in _WONDERS_, which had gone dark again—leaving a narrow yellow reflection in the wet lot where the long bird-tail crossed the grading plan. I put the twenties in the ashtray; the starter dragged once and caught on the second turn.

%%TITLE The Twelve-Pipe Fugue in Retrograde

They found the reel in the flooded basement annex of the conservatory in late October, sealed inside a rusted brass canister stamped #m[1872-TT1 — TWELVE PIPES — 48s].

I am an organist and a musicologist, not an archivist of ghosts. When the dean asked me to transcribe the tape for the winter recital program, I expected a Victorian curiosity—one of those clever, bloodless mirror-canons minor theorists wrote to prove they had read Bach's _Musical Offering_: a single twelve-note theme played on dry, reedy organ pipes against its own exact retrograde, both voices crossing cleanly at the twenty-fourth second and coming down together on a bare unison cadence at forty-eight.

Only when I timed the magnetic ribbon against my brass stopwatch in the practice loft did I notice the fault. The tape ran forty-nine seconds. In the closing bar —written plainly for six beats on the yellowing manuscript sheet tucked inside the canister—the retrograde voice arrived one count late. Six beats passed, the forward line closed cleanly on the tonic, I lifted my pencil, and a seventh beat entered in the space I had left clear at the end of the staff: slightly flat, hanging in the damp air like a footstep on a stair after everyone else has left the building.

I blamed the damp oxide ribbon and booked the old limestone chapel on the bluff above the gorge for civil twilight on a Friday evening—the only room in the county with a tracker-action positive organ whose twelve principal pipes matched the narrow temperament of the tape. I drew a small bracket under the two places where the hands crossed. In practice I could take either part away and still hear where it ought to be. I tried leaving one of the last notes out; I had to stop and check the pencil line. The omission had been harder to hear than the extra note.

%%CANON

The chapel was empty and cold. Through the lancet windows, November mist was rising off the dark river bottoms. I set the manuscript on the music desk, pulled the single eight-foot reed stop and a quiet sixteen-foot pedal, and began to play both hands at once—right hand forward, left hand in retrograde. At the twelfth second, the acoustics of the chapel changed. A forty-foot limestone nave should return a dry reed tone in less than a tenth of a second; instead, a note I had just released came back nearly four-tenths of a second late—deep, resonant, and slightly warm, as if the walls behind the choir stalls had opened into a cistern cut deep into the bluff. Beneath the floorboards, a steady sixty-nine-hertz hum began to answer my pedal note, vibrating up through my soles.

I kept my left hand going. At the twenty-fourth second, where my two thumbs met at the center of the keyboard, the loft grew unexpectedly warm and smelled of bruised quince and damp alder pollen under the varnish. Up in the high stonework beside the east window came three faint, dry clicks—like ceramic striking stone—and then stillness.

I reached the closing bar. I counted six beats aloud, lifted both hands cleanly from the manuals, and closed the stop. The organ pipes went silent. And then, half a breath later, the chapel itself played the seventh beat. It came from the far end of the dark nave, low enough for me to mistake it for a pedal—though both my feet were on the bench rail.

I gathered my manuscript off the desk with shaking fingers. Stuck to the back of the final page, sewn neatly along the left margin with waxed yellow linen thread I had not noticed in the conservatory basement, was a single leaf of older rag paper bearing three unmarked vocal lines—_Steward, Chorister, Stranger_—and a dry pencilled rubric at the bottom noting that the downstroke had been held, and that the dismissal was not yet due.

I locked the organ console, dropped the keys into my pocket, and left by the side vestry door, counting my footsteps in sixes down the fogged lane. When I reached the gravel and stopped walking to listen, the keys inside my pocket were still moving quietly against one another.

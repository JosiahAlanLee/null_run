%%TITLE The Man Who Kept the Ledger for the Glaciers

Every February, on the first clear morning after the river froze, old Martin Halvorsen walked the four-mile perimeter of Section 16 with a brass pocket compass, a birch walking-stick, and a county plat book bound in cracked calfskin.

He had been a land agent for forty years before he retired. To the east and north, where the glaciers had left twenty feet of scraped cobble and gravel, he could push his walking-stick through gravel deep enough to lose the point. On this side of the line red clay came away on the brass ferrule and the point struck bare dolomite. _Drift: nil_, the old 1872 ironworks boring logs read on every single hole from six chains west to fifty-two.

In an old deed Martin had found the phrase _seizin in fact_—the older, heavier law requiring a person to go out and stand on the land, because a piece of ground remembers everyone who ever stood on it and keeps its own books long after the courthouse burns. Every February he did.

He stopped at the southwest corner post of Section 16, a lichen-crusted block of dressed dolomite set into the ridge in October 1891. Twenty-five paces before he reached the stone, the red needle of his pocket compass drifted lazily off north and refused to settle. He slipped the compass back into his vest and laid his bare palm against the north face of the limestone outcrop beside the post. The air stood at sixteen degrees; the sun had not touched that north face since autumn. Under his palm, the dolomite was steadily, unmistakably warm.

West of the ruined ironworks foundation, low earth-banks followed the lip of the ridge in the long, quiet curve of spread wings pointing toward the river. Last autumn a county ditch crew had put out wooden stakes; their last stake still stood on the east side of the bank, just where Harris's shovels had stopped in 1872.

In unglaciated country, when the wind drops and the frost holds the oak leaves flat, time does not feel like a river running past you. It feels like a solid block of layered limestone—1872, 1891, 1953, and this cold morning all sitting side by side in the same bedding plane, separated by nothing thicker than tracing vellum. Halfway down the hollow, near the old dry stone sink, he heard something let down against the coping, and the faint clink of a chain dragged across frozen turf —always falling 3.60 chains short, pulling inward toward the fifth pin. Once, three winters ago, he had seen a thin man in a grey 1950s wool coat standing near the ironworks foundation with a tin dispatch box under his arm, checking a gauge whose needle never moved from 989 millibars; when Martin blinked against the snow-glare, there was only a red cedar.

Beneath his boots, deep in the lead-veined rock, three slow knocks sounded— _knock, knock, knock_—each separated from the next by a little more silence than he expected. Martin did not hurry away. He took the dry heel of his rye roll from his mackinaw pocket, set it neatly on the flat top of the southwest stone post, and held his hand there until the bread stood quite still.

At the northwest post he checked the frosted turf for the chain. There was no chain. Alongside the stone, the frost was broken in the shape of a small, empty coil. He went home by the lane, leaving Section 16 to keep its own balance in the dark.

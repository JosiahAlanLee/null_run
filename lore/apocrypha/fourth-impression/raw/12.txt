%%TITLE The Footnote That Outweighed the Folio

The book arrived at my bindery on a sleeting Tuesday in November, wrapped in oiled canvas and tied with butcher's twine that smelled faintly of cold iron and damp alder.

There was no cover letter—only a stamped requisition slip from an estates liquidator I had never heard of, requesting that the spine be re-sewn, the signatures pressed, and the whole returned before the quarter closed. At the bottom of the slip, in neat violet ink, someone had added a single instruction: _Do not brush the lower margin._

I untied the canvas on my oak bench beneath the green-shaded lamp. It was a privately printed 1898 critical edition of a two-act verse drama whose title page had been water-stained down to a pale, illegible ochre. The play itself occupied twelve thin leaves of laid rag paper. Act I was polite, faded decadence— three courtiers waiting in a lakeside pavilion for a host who would not arrive until the masks were laid aside. Act II broke off halfway down the ninth leaf, replaced by a dense lattice of editorial footnotes set in six-point Caslon.

The footnotes ran for two hundred and fourteen pages.

I have bound university theses, parish registers, and private occult pamphlets for thirty-five years, and I know how to tell a compositor's work from an annotator's. Yet this apparatus was different. Three hands had marked the same place in the text and argued with one another across the gutter, citing three mutually exclusive locations for the theater where the play had supposedly been staged—a subterranean gallery beneath twelve hundred metres of fresh lake water, a subglacial vault cut below seven hundred metres of ice, and a sealed limestone cavern where no daylight fell at any hour.

And buried inside Note 114—a footnote attached to a footnote explaining why the curtain cue was missing—sat a single loose leaf of heavier rag stock that had never been trimmed to the folio's edges. It was stamped #m[1953-B] in the upper-right corner, and its lower margin carried a thin, damp ridge of sweet-smelling yellow dust. When I lifted that single leaf off the bench to check its watermark, my wrist buckled.

The paste-pot rattled when I set it down. Paper does not weigh that much; a four-page signature of heavy laid rag weighs less than two ounces. I carried the single leaf flat in both hands across the shop to my cast-iron parcel scale, checked the beam against two one-pound brass tares, and laid 1953-B on the pan. I had to slide both brass poises all the way out to the extreme end of the beam before the iron tongue settled dead level.

Forty-one pounds, eight ounces. For one sheet of paper that bent lightly between my fingers when I picked it back up.

I took the leaf off and put the book itself on the pan. One pound, three ounces. I set the leaf on top of the book. The tongue held steady at one pound, three ounces. When I drew the book out from beneath it, the iron tongue struck the stop. I held the book by both boards and waited until the pan quit ringing.

I had quoted the liquidator by the gathering. Either the loose leaf was part of the gathering and the estimate stood, or I had taken in another job whose owner I could not name. I got out two repair tickets and wrote the same date on both. A bookbinder is a creature of habit, and habit is a remarkably sturdy armor against the impossible. I threaded the strongest needle in my drawer with waxed yellow linen and drew the first gathering tight enough to hold it square. When the second gathering settled, the loose leaf shifted level with the rest.

As I worked, I made the mistake of reading the final footnote at the bottom of page 214. It was not in the 1898 typeface. In a crisp, modern sans-serif, the note cited a binder's receipt dated the very Tuesday I was sitting at my bench. It gave the weight and the two one-pound tares, noted that the binder had sewn the folio anyway—thereby completing the second act without speaking a single line aloud—and then quoted the last entry on the receipt. I had left that line empty.

I stopped with the needle halfway through the kettle-stitch. Under the bench was a box of things I had taken out of books at their owners' request: pins, pressed grasses, a strip of ration coupons. I thought of putting the leaf in with them. I raised one board of the folio and slid the leaf halfway out. Three letters at the foot of the printed note went pale where the shadow of the leaf crossed them. The rest of the note was still dark enough to read.

I put the board down. From the street came the sound of a shoe turning on the wet bricks. Outside my rain-streaked shopfront, beneath the steady amber glow of the streetlamp, a tall figure in a dark pleated coat stood motionless on the pavement, facing away toward the street, no luggage at their feet, showing only a small square of stitched pale cloth pinned neatly between the shoulder blades.

I did not pull the needle back out. I tied off the yellow linen thread, pressed the boards firmly under the iron nipping-press until the paste set, and filled in the shipping tag with the only honest weight I could swear to—leaving the bottom entry waiting to be copied into the receipt. I left the shop lamp burning steady and locked the front door from the inside, knowing perfectly well that the folio on my bench was already heavier than the table holding it up.

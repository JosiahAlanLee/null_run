== I. Memorandum of Secondment (October 4, 1899)

For fourteen years in the British Museum Reading Room I had catalogued minor theological tracts and nineteenth-century provincial directories, content to leave the boundary between a real book and an invented one to the Copyright Act of 1842.

That illusion ended on a Tuesday morning when a pale gentleman from the Home Office—who introduced himself only as Mr. Venn and smelled faintly of damp binding-glue—summoned me to an unmarked office behind the King's Library and slid a telegraph slip across the blotter:

%%BLOCK wire

> FROM: NEW YORK STATION (CABLE)

> TO: CANONICAL OFFICE, LONDON

> ROOK SYNDICATE HAS ACQUIRED FULL GALLEY PROOFS OF THE MONTMARTRE TWO-ACT STOP SETTING TYPE IN JERSEY CITY LOFT ADJACENT TO WESTERN UNION REPEATER STOP PLANS TO TRANSMIT ACT II AS SERIALIZED MORSE WIRE NOV 1 STOP IF IT ENTERS THE CABLE THE TEXT WILL NOT STAY ON PAPER STOP SEND MARGINALS IMMEDIATELY

"Caleb Rook has filled railway stalls with pirated novels for ten years," Venn said. "Usually we leave him to the lawyers. The French prefect thought he had burned these proofs in '93. Rook has bought a second set, and he intends to run the second act straight through the transatlantic copper."

"Forgive me, sir," I said. "Why me? And what on earth are _Marginals_?"

"You've spent fourteen years seeing things that don't belong in the catalogue, Crabb. My men put down the first proof and asked for a prompt-book. Real men read the second act and lose their minds. You send people who are already half-ink."

== II. Dossier of the Committee (Assembled at Liverpool, October 9, 1899)

We sailed aboard the _Cunardia_ in second-class cabins booked under false imprints. I was instructed to keep a formal log of my four companions, though none of them treated the Ministry with anything resembling respect.

1. *Henriette Delacroix (age 34, Paris).* Formerly a minor figure in a suppressed 1887 symbolist anthology whose publisher threw the entire print run into the Seine. Sharper in gaslight than at noon, permanently smelling of wet newsprint and absinthe, and capable of memorizing any text she touches with her bare fingertips. She regards the Ministry as "a committee of customs officers trying to tax the fog."

2. *Dr. Otto Breuer (age 58, Vienna).* A retired alienist whose sole claim to notoriety was treating a celebrated fictional sleepwalker later excised from the second edition of a German Gothic romance. Because his patient was cut by the editor, Breuer's clinic ceased to exist on any Viennese street map in 1884. Carries a black leather valise full of laudanum, brass tuning forks, and marginalia on infectious prose.

3. *Tamsin Trevithick (age 29, Redruth / Mineral Point).* Daughter of a Cornish mine-captain who emigrated to the Wisconsin lead diggings in the seventies. Survived the collapse of an unmapped adit by bargaining with the _knockers_—the underground tapping spirits the miners leave crust-ends for—and walked out three days later carrying a lead lantern that burns without oil. Technically she belongs to folklore rather than print, making her the only one of us who can cross a threshold without being rewritten by it.

4. *Bram (age unknown, formerly of the Théâtre-Libre, Paris).* A mute stagehand who struck the scenery after the sole private performance of the banned two-act play in Montmartre in 1891. Survived because he never read the prompt-book—he only carried the yellow backdrops. Since that night he casts no shadow under gaslight, and his knuckles leave faint ochre smudges on white linen.

"A charming little remainder-bin," Henriette remarked on our third night at sea, blowing cigarette smoke at the porthole. "A cut chapter, a deleted footnote, a folk-tale, a stagehand, and a librarian who still thinks he has a birth certificate. Tell me, Mr. Crabb—did Mr. Venn tell you what happens to us if we actually stop Rook?"

"We return to London and receive our stipends," I said stiffly.

Dr. Breuer chuckled from his bunk, tapping a tuning fork against his knee. "Oh, my dear fellow. Have you ever checked the parish register for your own baptism?" I did not answer him. I went up on deck and stayed in the freezing spray until my hands went numb.

== III. Field Report: The Jersey City Pressroom (October 29, 1899)

We reached Hoboken in a driving sleet and crossed to the brick warehouse Rook had leased three doors down from the Western Union cable-head in Jersey City. At street level I could see two courses of windows—a building bigger on the inside than its brick frontage allowed—yet the iron stair behind the printing shop climbed past four landings where there should only have been two, opening into a vaulted composing room lit by sputtering yellow arc-lamps.

Caleb Rook stood beside a row of three Linotype machines, a thick-necked man in an ink-stained waistcoat and smoked spectacles. Thick gutta-percha telegraph cables had been tapped illegally through the brickwork and clamped directly to the brass matrices of the typesetting frames.

"Too late, London," Rook called out, his voice echoing back half a beat slow from rafters we couldn't see. "Act One is already on the wire to Chicago and Liverpool. In ten minutes my operators key the first scene of Act Two. Five hundred newspaper offices wake up tomorrow with #struck sitting right on the front page. Do you know what the circulation will be?"

"It won't be circulation, you fool," Dr. Breuer snapped, his tuning fork raised. "It will be _annexation_. Ask one of your operators what he can see behind his key."

Rook laughed and reached for the brass telegraph key. Tamsin Trevithick brought the heel of her unlit lead lantern straight down onto the cast-iron floorboards. Three knocks answered from underneath—the sharp, rhythmic _knock-knock-knock_ of a Cornish hammer on deep limestone—and the floorboards beneath the Linotype machines sagged six inches, snapping two of the illegally tapped cables in a shower of blue sparks.

Rook lunged for the third key. Bram stepped silently between them. Under the yellow arc-lamps his shadow did not cross the floor. He unfolded a strip of faded yellow canvas straight into the old crease between Rook's face and the brass matrices, as if striking a flat between scenes.

Rook froze. His smoked spectacles slid off his nose and shattered on the floor. For three seconds, the composing room smelled of wet stone and dead reeds, and something vast and impatient leaned against the far wall of the warehouse, rattling every lead type-tray in the racks.

Henriette seized the galley proof of Act II from the stone—ignoring how the wet ink blistered her bare palms—dropped it into the kerosene-soaked rag bin, and put her cigarette to it. The paper burned a thick, oily yellow, like a distant crowd sighing in disappointment. The printer heard the fire before he turned to see it; he called for someone in the next room, and by the time he turned the room was gone.

When the smoke cleared, we were standing in an ordinary, drafty Jersey City loft twenty feet wide, beside three jammed Linotype machines and a weeping printer who could no longer remember his own middle name.

== IV. Postscript: Exhibit Attached to Back-Matter (November 12, 1899)

I filed my report upon returning to Whitehall and requested reassignment to the Reading Room. Mr. Venn thanked me warmly, locked my dossier in a green tin box, and suggested a week's holiday in Brighton. He made one mistake: he left the Ministry's internal ledger open on his desk while fetching a receipt. I am a cataloguer by training; I read upside-down without thinking. It was a publisher's dummy titled _Catalogue of Apocryphal Persons Retained as Counterweights (London, 1885–1900)_. On page forty-two, right beneath my four companions, stood this notice in neat 8-point Caslon:

%%BLOCK entry

> *CRABB, EDMUND.* _Assistant Keeper of Printed Books (fict.). Originally drafted as narrator of an unfinished 1884 ghost story by an anonymous Lincoln's Inn barrister; rescued from estate waste-paper by the Ministry. Useful for dry documentary framing and border maintenance. Note: Subject still believes himself born in Ipswich in 1861. Do NOT allow subject to consult the Suffolk parish registers, as awareness of his own uncollected status tends to loosen the binding of the file._

I did not go to Brighton. I took the afternoon train to Ipswich and walked straight into the vestry of St. Margaret's. The 1861 baptismal register is intact, clean, and meticulously indexed. My name is not in it. Between _Crampton, Josiah_ and _Crisp, Hannah_, there is only a narrow blank margin where the clerk's pen skipped —and pressed into the gutter of the binding, a single grain of yellow printer's dust.

I sent a telegram to Henriette in Paris and one to Tamsin in Wisconsin. Sooner or later, the margins begin to write back. Then I paid for a reply addressed to myself. The clerk asked me to write the full name. I stood at his rail while he entered it.

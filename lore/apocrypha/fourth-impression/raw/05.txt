%%TITLE The Clerk of Unwritten Drafts

== I. Aisle 409 (Unfinished — Temperance & Melancholy)

The trouble with mortals is that they think an archive is a place where dead things are kept quiet. They have never worked the night shift in the Sleeping Stacks.

My name is Simeon, and for four hundred and twelve years— give or take a decade during the Napoleonic Wars, when the clocks in the East Wing melted into tallow—I have been an Assistant Shelver in the Department of Unwritten Drafts. Every novel a mortal abandons on page nineteen, every letter burned in shame over a kitchen sink, every epic poem composed in the shower and forgotten before the towel is dry eventually drifts down through the ceiling vents onto our returns carts.

We catalogue them, bind them in soft grey buckram, and shelve them alongside the waking world's real editions so the floorboards stay level. There is a tenderness to a book nobody ever had the courage to finish; it does not shout, it does not demand royalties, and it never tries to climb off the shelf.

Until Tuesday, when the yellow pamphlet landed on Cart 14. An unwritten book is light as balsa wood—all intention and no ballast. This slim, paper-wrapped octavo weighed nearly eleven pounds. It sagged my wrist toward the floorboards, and the ochre paper felt warm and slightly greasy, like an old theatre program kept too close to a radiator. When I tipped the faded gilt into the light I could make out a cast of three, a ball beside a dark lake, and a king whose entrance was printed after the curtain.

I checked the intake ledger: no entry. Burned-in-Shame annex: nothing. Then I checked the *Citation Index*, and my stomach dropped. The Citation Index had three drawers of references to it. I pulled a provincial review first: its quotation began on page nineteen. The occult pamphlet under it cited nineteen as well, though its author admitted he had never seen a copy. The book had never been written _or_ unwritten—only quoted across a century of hearsay until borrowed belief precipitated a physical copy. I closed the drawer. By then I was holding one.

By the time I reached the Chief Archivist's desk to file a Quarantine Writ, yellow ink was seeping through the grey buckram of neighboring volumes on Cart 14. An abandoned 1954 romance from Duluth had lost its third chapter to neat italicized stage directions instructing the heroine to lay aside her coat and listen for the seventh beat of the organ. A half-written parish history from Sauk County had sprouted a cast list on its flyleaf. Worse, the cedar floorboards beneath the cart were softening into wet limestone, and the warm, sweet smell of out-of-season alder pollen was drifting down Aisle 409.

"You cannot incinerate a hearsay text," the Chief Archivist said, not looking up from his magnifying glass. He had been made out of the margin-notes of three lost Alexandrian rolls, and hated paperwork even more than contagion. "Burn it here, and the smoke goes straight back up the vents into forty thousand bedrooms. Someone up there is acting as the press—reading fragments in the dark and dreaming the missing second act onto our shelves. Follow the pressure back. And Simeon, take a coat. It is November in Wisconsin."

== II. The Rain-Door (3:14 AM, U.S. Highway 12)

I opened the utility closet behind Periodicals, stepped over the mop bucket, and put my shoulder to the steel fire door. Diesel exhaust came through before the light did. I came out behind a vending machine in a Greyhound bus depot on the edge of a wet Wisconsin town at three-fourteen in the morning.

Only one person was waiting in the depot: a woman in her late thirties wearing faded hospital scrubs under a damp fleece jacket, worn thin in the way people get when they spend twelve hours a night typing up other people's diagnoses. Her ballpoint pen moved across a cheap spiral notebook in a tight, rhythmic slant that never paused at the margins. Every time her pen reached the bottom of a page, the fluorescent tube overhead buzzed a half-tone flatter, and a faint smear of yellow dust drifted out of the heating grate by her boots.

I smoothed my borrowed grey wool coat and sat down two seats away. "You're writing the wrong half," I said quietly. "Act Two. The second-to-last line has come through three shelves. What have you got at the bottom of the page?"

"Maren," she said, almost to herself. "Wait—who are you?"

"Simeon. Assistant Shelver, Department of Unwritten Drafts." I laid a dry index card stamped in violet ink (_RECALL NOTICE — UNCATALOGUED CONTAGION_) on the plastic seat. "You don't have to believe I'm real, Maren. Just look at your fingers."

Packed beneath the nail of her right index finger, where she gripped the Bic pen, was a bright crescent of damp yellow pollen. Her breath hitched. She pressed her palm flat against the cover as if holding down a lid. "Every time I closed my eyes after a double shift, I kept hearing this cadence—like a play someone was rehearsing in the empty apartment next door. I thought if I wrote it down, it would get out of my head."

Beneath our feet the tiles made the small two-part sound a shelf makes just before it gives. The glass doors rattled once in vertical, windless rain. Out by the bus bay, in the amber wash of the sodium lamp, a tall figure in a wet, ochre-colored raincoat stood motionless with its back to the street, facing the depot glass. It had no luggage.

"How do I stop it?" Maren whispered. "Burn the notebook?"

"No. Burning only sends it down to my department. You have to trade it."

== III. The Exchange Rate

The form offered two lines: _weight removed_ and _weight left_. It is the only form in the Stacks a shelver is expected to sign before filling in either line, because the Stacks obey conservation of weight.

"I need a memory you haven't lost yet," I told her. "Not a bad one—bad memories are light; everybody tries to unload them. Something small and specific you still use to orient yourself. The layout of a house you grew up in. The taste of a winter morning before you knew what grief was."

Maren looked out at the figure in the yellow raincoat. It had taken one slow step closer to the glass. Even through the closed doors, I smelled wet dolomite and bruised quince over the depot's bleach. "If I give it to you, what happens to the play?"

"I file it under _Non-Existent, Verified_," I said. "I bind it in lead-lined buckram beside three unwritten treatises by Giordano Bruno and a love letter Franz Kafka never posted. And you go home and sleep. When you wake there will be a particular place you can still name; getting there in your mind will be harder."

She unscrewed the cap of her pen, tore the filled pages cleanly out of the wire coil, and held them out to me. "Take the summer I was nine," she said steadily. "My grandfather's screened porch in Baraboo. The way the cedar boards smelled after a storm, and the exact creak the third step made when I snuck out before dawn to watch the mist come off the river. Keep it somewhere dry."

I took the torn pages. Instantly they grew heavy in my palm—eleven pounds of damp hearsay collapsing back into quiet ink—while a warm scent of wet cedar and river-mist slid into my coat pocket, folding itself into a weightless chapbook. Outside, the sodium light faded to a dull amber. When the rain cleared the pane, the bus bay was empty.

Maren blinked and let out a long, ragged yawn, staring at the empty notebook with mild confusion. "Did I miss the 3:30 to Madison?"

"It's running five minutes late," I said, buttoning my grey coat. "You have plenty of time."

Back in the Sleeping Stacks, I locked Act II in the lead-lined cabinet at the end of Aisle 409, signed the Quarantine Writ, and checked the floorboards: solid wood again. Before going off shift, I bound Maren's cedar porch in clean blue linen without a spine title and shelved it in the East Wing where the air stays dry, between an unmailed apology from 1919 and a lullaby nobody has sung since the last glacier melted, and turned down the lamp.

%%TITLE The Unfinished Room at the End of the Corridor

Before they condemned the east wing of the St. Jude Sanatorium, the historical society hired me to photograph every corridor on medium-format film. It was honest, cold work: peeling institutional plaster, quarry-red herringbone tile laid in 1924, heavy oak doors swollen shut by forty winters of river damp. I worked alone from seven until noon, setting my tripod on the exact centerline of each hall so the vanishing points would stay clean.

I noticed the error in the darkroom on Thursday night. I had wound on once in the third-floor east corridor from the stairwell doorway, looking straight down toward a blank cinderblock bulkhead two hundred and sixteen feet away. On the contact sheet, however, two images sat inside the same frame line. They shared the hook of lint in the lower-left corner and the discarded ticket beside the tripod leg. In one the bulkhead was a clean rectangle. In the other, six feet past where the wall should have stood, the red herringbone tile continued cleanly under an open arch into a low, dim chamber lit by a single amber bulkhead lamp—and the ticket beside my tripod carried the shadow of the lamp from beyond it. I put that strip in an envelope by itself and left the negative number in pencil.

I blamed a double exposure—until I went back on Friday morning with a steel tape and a pair of work gloves. The third-floor corridor was empty, smelling of floor wax and wet lime. Yet halfway down the hall, two pale masonry scars ran straight across the herringbone floor where partition walls had been struck and their sills grubbed out (cf. Works Order 53/119). Beyond the second scar, the plaster ended abruptly.

On the left wall stood a wide, riveted steel window frame pressed flush against deep, motionless green water—green all the way to the sill, with pale silt banked along the bottom edge of the glass. Six feet further down the same wall, a bank of iron ventilation louvres was sealed across its third row with crisp white rime, breathing a dry polar chill. And beyond the louvres, the corridor wall ceased to be masonry at all, opening onto raw, tool-marked limestone studded with tiny spiral marine fossils. Three incompatible worlds bolted onto one continuous hallway, and not a single seam leaked.

At the far end of the corridor, beneath a steady amber bulkhead lamp whose two braided cloth leads hung completely loose and unconnected in the air, a slight man in a damp wool coat sat on a folding wooden stool. A brass stopwatch lay open in his palm. "You're six feet early," he said politely, without looking up from the dial. "The demolition crew isn't scheduled until Monday."

I took out the envelope and held the contact strip against my sleeve. In the print he held the watch in his right hand; the man in front of me had his right hand resting on his knee.

I backed out with my tripod and turned in the contact sheets on Saturday morning. They tore down the east wing the following Tuesday. When I drove past the rubble lot in the rain on Wednesday afternoon, the bricks were stacked for salvage, and at the center of the muddy foundation slab the rain gave off a little steam from a single untouched square of warm, dry limestone, with no tracks leading away from it. I had gloves in the car. I went back for them.

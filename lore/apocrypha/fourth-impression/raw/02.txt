%%TITLE The Four-Dimensional Plat of Hawksmoor County

== I. The Departure from Mineral Point (December 14, 1891)

The snow had stopped before dawn, leaving the Driftless ridges striped like old pewter—white along the crests, iron-grey in the limestone cuts where the wind stripped the talus bare. At half past seven, Ezra Pinchot climbed into the hired brougham outside the Mineral Point land office, tapped the roof twice with his brass-ferruled level rod, and told the driver to take the old Military Ridge track west.

I sat opposite him with my mahogany portfolio across my knees. I was twenty-four, six months out of the engineering school in Madison, and still foolish enough to believe that a county surveyor's business was acreage, drainage, and the honest settlement of boundary disputes.

"Put the transit away, Thomas," Pinchot said without looking at me. He was seventy-one, gaunt as a winter heron, with the chalk-colored skin of a man who had spent forty winters in unheated courthouse vaults. "We are not measuring the surface today. We are checking the pins."

"The section corners, sir?"

"No," he said, drawing a folded linen plat from his ulster. "The five nails that hold the county down."

He spread the map across our knees. The roads and townships of the plat I'd helped ink that autumn were faint sepia. Over them, in dense iron-gall black, he had drawn a course between four dots. The fifth dot was inside the course. None of the rules met it.

"Here is the gravel," Pinchot said. His glove moved across the linen. "Here too. We bored at every mark. On this ridge the auger went through red clay and into rock." He tapped the linen with a gloved knuckle. "The line goes round," he said. "That is what I intend to check."

== II. First Pin: The Smelting Chimney on Shake Rag

We stopped ten minutes later above a narrow draw where the Cornish miners had dug their badger-holes in the 1830s. Most of the old cabins had sagged into the mud, but on the east lip of the ravine stood a forty-foot octagonal smelting chimney built of dressed Galena dolomite, blackened inside by half a century of lead smoke and dead since the sixties.

Pinchot did not get out of the carriage. He lowered the glass and pointed with his rod. "Built in 1849 by a mason from Truro who disappeared three days after the capstone was set. Look at the flue. The prevailing wind is southwest. The opening faces sixteen degrees east of north." On the linen the rule ran straight to a low bank seven miles up the ridge, entered in the county book as wash.

"A bad draft," I said.

"Half a century of work for a bad draft." Pinchot sighted along his rod. "Hold a plumb line across that rule. It will keep falling when it reaches the edge of the paper."

"Sideways into what?"

"Through the date, Thomas. Yesterday is the room behind the wainscoting. Take 1849. It is sitting six inches to your left, with the furnace still lit and the mason still mortaring the flue." He rapped the roof. The carriage lurched forward.

== III. Second and Third Pins: The Courthouse and the Octagonal Barn

By eleven o'clock we had passed the stone courthouse in Dodgeville—where Pinchot made me count the thirteen blind niches under the cornice that no builder's invoice ever billed for—and climbed the long wind-scoured spine of the ridge toward an abandoned stone barn near Ridgeway.

It was a queer, eight-sided structure of yellow-buff limestone, set far too high on the bluff for sensible stock-keeping, with narrow lancet slits instead of hayloft doors. A rusted lightning rod rose from its cupola, bent slightly to the west as if something heavy had leaned against it.

"Built in 1871," Pinchot said, stepping out at last into the biting wind. "By a Norwegian farmer whose stock return lists six cows. He finished the roof on All Hallows' Eve and walked into the timber the next morning without his boots. There are thirteen blind niches here too. Count them from the tool marks."

He walked to the base of the north wall and pressed his bare palm against the yellow limestone. "Touch it, Thomas."

I hesitated, then pulled off my glove and laid my hand beside his. The air temperature was perhaps twelve degrees above zero. The stone was warm—not hot, merely the steady, unsettling warmth of a parlor wall with a hearth burning on the far side.

"You feel that?" Pinchot whispered. "That is not the sun. The sun hasn't touched this north face since September. You are feeling the _other_ building that occupies this exact plat."

I pulled my hand back. My fingers smelled faintly of wet chalk and crushed yellow clover. "What other building?"

"A city. A court, perhaps. In the eighties a little yellow pamphlet came out of Paris that put a name to it—#struck, they called it, though names are merely handles for the illiterate." Pinchot looked down at our two hands on the wall and then back at the linen. "I can draw the buildings on the bearings."

He smiled, and for the first time I noticed how little resemblance his smile bore to human mirth. It was the satisfied grin of a clerk whose columns have finally balanced.

He handed me the linen to copy the niches. I set my dividers to the first pair of tool marks. The points went through two holes already in the cloth. At the courthouse, before eleven, he had stood behind me and held the linen taut while I pricked those holes. I tried the next pair. He waited, watching the tips.

"All those years of marking improvements," he said. "Will you initial this one for me?" I kept my glove in my hand.

== IV. Fourth and Fifth Pins: The Tollhouse and the Dry Cistern

I should have bolted at the Ridgeway barn. Partly it was the cold—we were nine miles from any livery—and partly it was the awful fascination of watching a sane, meticulous mind assemble an impossible proof out of real bricks and county deeds.

We passed the fourth pin at two in the afternoon: an empty limestone tollhouse beside a washed-out ferry slip near Arena, its windows bricked shut with yellow firebrick from the inside. Pinchot barely glanced at it. He was watching the western sky, where the low December sun had begun to smear the clouds the color of old tallow.

"Four pins hold the edges," he said as the road climbed into the bluffs. "A fifth holds the level. For that the county requires a witness who understands the survey."

The brougham stopped where the track narrowed to a cedar-choked ravine. Below us, the river lay half-frozen under a lid of pewter fog. Fifty yards up the limestone draw sat a low circular wall of dressed stone, perhaps twelve feet across: an old dry cistern built for a hilltop orchard that had burned in 1882.

Pinchot climbed down and beckoned me forward. The driver did not look back; he sat hunched on the box with his collar up, staring straight ahead at the horses' ears as if he had been paid extra not to turn his head.

When I reached the rim of the cistern and looked down, my breath caught in my throat. The pit was dry, and its floor was maybe ten feet below the coping—yet when I looked straight down, the stone sides seemed to drop away for a hundred yards, ringed with tiered yellow-marble galleries that caught the faint reflection of two pale suns hanging low over a dark lake. At the very center of the floor, directly beneath my boots, a tall figure sat motionless on a throne of raw, unsmelted galena, draped in heavy ochre linen that shifted slightly in a wind I could not feel on my face.

I tried to step back. My heels would not lift from the frozen turf. "The fifth spire," Pinchot said. "End on." He sounded like a man at the other end of a long room.

"Why did you bring me?" I whispered. My tongue felt thick with lead dust.

"Because a survey is not legally closed until the chainman drives the final benchmark," Pinchot said gently, taking my mahogany portfolio from under my arm and laying it neatly on the stone coping. "I am seventy-one. My lines are shaky. You are twenty-four, your eyes are sharp, and you have already signed the field book for all four corners. A living benchmark holds a fourth-dimensional plat far better than iron."

He raised his brass-ferruled level rod—not to strike me, merely to sight along it toward the dimming west, where the tallow light was thickening into something solid and bright. "Look down, Thomas," he murmured. "He is taking off his mask, and the county is finally level."

== Appendix: Extracts from the Iowa & Crawford County Plat Books

*1. Iowa County Sheriff's Register, Folio 19 (December 18, 1891):*_Missing person: Thomas Vane, age 24, junior draftsman employed by E. Pinchot, County Surveyor. Last seen departing Mineral Point in a hired brougham on the morning of Dec. 14. Driver (H. Miller) reports Mr. Pinchot returned alone to the carriage near the old orchard cistern above the river at dusk, stating Mr. Vane had elected to walk down to the ferry landing. Search of the ravine yielded only a mahogany portfolio resting on the cistern coping. Cistern measured nine feet, eight inches deep; empty save for dry oak leaves and a small vein of exposed galena._

*2. Note appended in pencil to the 1892 County Resurvey (Township 7 North, Range 3 East) by J. R. Halstead, Deputy Surveyor:*_Cannot make Pinchot's 1891 traverse close. Running the bearings from the Shake Rag chimney to the Dodgeville courthouse, the Ridgeway octagonal barn, and the Arena tollhouse produces a consistent closure error of 4.7 chains__—always inward, toward the empty section above the river. Re-chained twice in clear weather with a new steel tape. Same discrepancy. Recommend leaving Pinchot's monument undisturbed; the compass needle refuses to settle within fifty paces of the old cistern, and the rock there is uncommonly warm for January._

%%TITLE The Mineral Springs at Intermission

== I. The Palace Springs Hotel (October 1913)

By late October, the Palace Springs Hotel had narrowed itself down to a single lighted parlor, a coal grate that smoked when the wind came off the lake, and three guests who had no intention of taking the waters.

It was a rambling Gilded Age pile of yellow-painted timber and peeling verandas built in the 1880s around a sulfur spring on the Wisconsin shore, back when Chicago doctors still prescribed mineral baths for nervous women and exhausted financiers. Now the bathhouse roof leaked, the wicker chairs on the porch stood stacked under canvas, and the only staff visible after supper was a narrow, quiet bellboy in faded ochre livery who looked nineteen and moved as if he had been carrying luggage down these corridors since before the railroad reached the county.

The three of us found each other on the first evening in the conservatory, drawn by the green bottle of Swiss absinthe Julian Marsh had smuggled through customs in his hatbox. We were all in our late thirties or early forties—well past the age when anyone believes what they were told in the nursery. Clara Venn was a sharp-boned woman from Lincolnshire who wore dark wool and smoked Turkish cigarettes down to the cork. Julian Marsh had the slightly bruised look of an art dealer who had spent twenty years buying forged miniatures to pay for a taste he couldn't name. I, Hester Cole, had come up from Milwaukee with a suitcase of mourning clothes I no longer needed and a persistent ache behind my ribs that no physician could find by pressing on them.

It was Julian who broke the politeness on the second night, while rain drummed against the conservatory glass and the sugar cubes dissolved into pale opalescent cloud in our glasses.

"They always leave out the smell," he said abruptly, staring at the wet black panes.

Clara paused with her match halfway to her cigarette. "Who does?"

"The people who write the books," Julian said. "The clergymen and the bachelor dons who turn it into a moral lesson for the schoolroom. They remember the crystal towers. What I remember is sweat and crushed bay leaves and warm brass. And they never tell you what it feels like to be thrown back through the door when your voice breaks."

The room went very still. A coal shifted in the grate. Clara blew out her match and looked at Julian, then at me, with startled recognition. "How old were you?"

"Fourteen," Julian said, and drank half his glass. "A folio encyclopedia in my uncle's library in Bath. Plate XLII: _A View of the Southern Marches_. If you pressed your palm flat against the copperplate engraving after midnight, the paper went wet and warm, and you could pull yourself right through the margin onto a quay beside a lake with two suns."

"And what did it cost you?" I asked softly.

Julian gave a short, bitter laugh. "For everything I wished for I left a memory on the stones. By the time I was fourteen, I couldn't remember my mother's Christian name, or the color of the wallpaper in my bedroom, or what snow felt like.

== II. What the Chaperones Edited Out

Clara leaned back into the velvet armchair and crossed her legs. There was nothing dreamy or wistful in her face; she looked like a woman auditing an old debt.

"A pier-glass in a vicarage garret," she said. "My father was a canon with six daughters and no money. When I was eleven, I found that if I breathed on the silvering in the upper-left corner, the glass softened like tallow. On the other side was a winter orchard where the trees bore red glass fruit and a tall, gray-skinned steward kept court in a ruined greenhouse."

"Did he try to eat you?" I asked.

"God, no," Clara said, exhaling a thin stream of smoke. "That's the fairy-tale version—everything neat and monstrous so the mothers won't panic. He _worshipped_ me, Hester. He knelt in the black mud and kissed the mud off my boots and told me I was the only honest thing in a dead country. For four years I could count on him being there. And when I turned fifteen—the exact week my body began to change, the week I stopped wanting to be a child-queen and started wanting to pull him up off his knees and bite his collarbone—the mirror froze solid."

She tapped her ash into the saucer. Her hand was entirely steady, though a flush had risen along her throat. "Do you know what it does to a woman," she said quietly, "to be trained for four years by an ancient, starving court to expect an appetite that size—and then to be locked out in Lincolnshire and told to marry a solicitor who turns off the gas-lamp before he unbuttons his collar?"

Julian turned his glass in its wet ring. I could hear the grate drawing. "And you, Hester?" Clara asked. "You didn't come to a dying sulfur-spring hotel in October for your lungs."

"No," I said. I set my glass down on the marble table. "Mine wasn't a mirror or a book. It was a chalk hollow behind my grandfather's sheep-fold in Sussex. A man with a bone reed-pipe sat on the stile at dusk. In the books, the piper is supposed to make you forget—a mercy, they call it, so your little human heart doesn't break."

I unbuttoned the cuff of my dark wool sleeve and pushed it back to the elbow. Even in the dim parlor light, the faint pale ridges were visible across the inside of my forearm—dozens of tiny, crescent-shaped marks, like the imprints of fingernails pressed in during a fever.

"He didn't make me forget," I said. "He made me _stay_ for what felt like three summers while six hours passed on the downs. There was no castle, no throne, no moral. Just a warm limestone cavern full of people in tattered yellow linen dancing until their shoes wore through, and a music that took hold of the meat of you and shook every polite word out of your head. At dawn I came up the rabbit-burrow with my shift soaked with dew and wine, and my grandmother beat me with a hearth-brush for ruining my boots. The dew dried. The wine left a stain."

I looked up at both of them. "I married a dry-goods merchant in Milwaukee. He died last winter. He was a kind man. For fifteen years I lay beside him in the dark and listened to the lake wind and wished—God help me—that someone would drag me back down into the chalk and never let me come up clean."

== III. The Bellboy's Key

By the third night we knew which parts of the stories each of us had held back. Julian had the girl's place on the steps exactly. Clara could go round her orchard tree by tree. I remembered a tune and was afraid to hum it.

At midnight on the third night, while the wind rattled the shuttered verandas and the absinthe bottle stood empty on the tray, the door to the conservatory opened without a knock. It was the bellboy in the faded ochre livery. He carried a small tarnished brass tray. On it lay a single heavy iron key, an unsealed envelope of thick laid paper, and three small sprigs of yellow alder-catkins—fresh, damp with pollen, six months out of season.

"The management sends its compliments," the bellboy said. His voice was neither young nor old; it sounded like a cello string plucked in an empty stairwell. "Suite 400 is now ready."

Julian frowned. "This hotel only has three floors."

"It had three floors when you checked in, sir," the bellboy replied politely. "It has been waiting for the three of you to finish comparing notes."

Clara stood up slowly. She picked up one of the yellow sprigs from the tray and rolled the stem between her thumb and forefinger, smearing bright ochre dust across her pale skin. "Why now?" she asked him. "They locked us out when we grew up. They said we were too old."

The bellboy set the envelope down. It was damp only at the corners. "The management has entered three returns, madam. This is the first evening all three have been in order." He lifted the iron key and held it out into the warm, smoky air between our three chairs. "The conservatory upstairs has three doors. The orchard, the quay, and the chalk hollow are marked on the card. Breakfast is at seven."

I looked at Julian. His eyes were bright and wet, the bruised tiredness gone from his face as if a lamp had been lit behind his ribs. Clara had already dropped her cigarette into the saucer and was reaching for the key.

Outside, the Wisconsin lake hammered against the pier in the dark—cold, honest, and entirely flat. Up the grand staircase, somewhere above the third-floor landing where no fourth floor had stood an hour ago, a bone reed-pipe drew a single, long, brassy note that made the glass in the conservatory hum.

Clara took the key. Julian offered me his arm, his hand burning through the wool of my sleeve. I went back for my coat. When I returned, both of them were waiting on the first tread. Without looking back at our luggage, we went up together.

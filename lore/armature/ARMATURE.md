# THE ARMATURE — the reinforcement that stood up

**Status: PROPOSAL throughout.** Common-mob sheet. I have not seen the image; §1 is Claude's description,
quoted. Origin proposed **never human**: a tied rebar cage that learned to stand. No flesh, no gore, no
voice.

## 1. What the description establishes

| Description detail | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| 1.8 m humanoid | Human height, human proportions. At distance, in the dark, it can be mistaken for a person. That is the first scare and the reason it is the common mob. |
| Built entirely of rusted rebar bundles | Not a skeleton wearing armour: the bars are the body. Each limb is a bundle, not a single rod. |
| Bound with tie wire | The wire is the only thing holding the bundle together. It is the creature's joints, bindings and mortality at once. |
| Hollow, cage-ribbed torso | A rib cage of bars with nothing inside. Light passes through it. The emptiness is the point. |
| Wire-wrapped knees and elbows | The hinges are wraps, not joints. They bend in small steps and creak under load. |
| Long rebar fingers | Long bars, too long for a human hand, without knuckles. For reaching and hooking, not for tools. |
| Featureless rebar-lattice head | No face, no eyes, no mouth. The head is a cage; the player reads orientation, not intention. |
| Poses: walk, windup, strike, hit reaction | The four states the fight needs. Everything else — idle, notice, turn, lope, death, heap — is derived. |

Three facts: **it is a bundle**, **it is tied**, **it stands**. The first is the material; the second is the
mortality; the third is the encounter.

## 3. Lore proposals

### 3.1 Origin (never human)

It is what the works department says it is, and that is the horror. A cage of starter bars was tied and
left standing when the works were suspended — a column cage, a wall starter, a bundle against a scaffold.
The pour never came. The bars kept the shape they were tied into long enough that the shape became a habit,
and the habit became a body. It did not grow; it was assembled, and then it noticed it was assembled.
There is no ritual here and no maker's hand at the moment of waking: the workers tied a cage and left, and
the cage did the rest. It is at the edge of organic — not alive in the way a body is alive, but not inert
either; a habit in a material, the way a bent bar keeps a bend.

This is the first rung of the setting's material awareness, and the reason it is the most common creature:
**it has learned one verb, stand, and one response, test the load.** It is not a person, not a ghost, not a
golem, not a haunted suit of armour. There is no worker inside the cage. The emptiness is the whole point,
and the record's flat classification is correct: it is left-over reinforcement that stood up. The form
contains the correct sentence and then the word UNRESOLVED, because the office can write "stood up" in a
description but not in an origin.

### 3.2 What it wants (proposal)

To keep the shape it was tied into. It stands where the cage stood; it resists load; it moves when the load
changes. The player is a load — not a person, not prey. When the player comes near, the Armature turns to
face the change, walks to test it, and strikes to see how it takes the blow. The windup is a **load test**:
the arm draws back, the bindings tighten, the creak rises. The strike is the test. If the load leaves, the
test is over; it does not follow beyond the room, because the room is the extent of the load path it was
tied into.

This gives the creature a complete, non-human motive and keeps it from being a zombie. It is not angry, not
hungry, not imitating a person. It is **doing its job in a building that has forgotten the job**. The
office's word for the species is **standing reinforcement**; the trade word is **standers**. THE ARMATURE
is the name the player learns; the office never uses it. The heap is the only document it leaves, and the
office files the heap as surplus.

**Numbers rules.**
- The default pack is **three**. Two is a pair; five is a stand; six is the set-piece maximum. Never more
  than six in one encounter.
- One loper per four Armatures, rounded down; a pack of five has one. The loper is a state variant, not a
  second actor.
- Density in circulation (corridors, service routes, open floors): one per 100 m² of shadowed floor; one per
  300 m² of lit floor. The room table is the encounter budget for set-piece rooms; do not stack both.
- A large room uses two separate packs of three, separated by a dark divider and waking independently,
  rather than one pack of six.
- Never mix a pack with another pack creature in the same room. A single Armature may share a room with a
  larger set-piece creature (a Pleat at a fitting, a Span at a gap) as the smaller load.
- Heaps do not count against the cap. A room with three heaps and two standing Armatures is the intended
  read: the room has a history.
- The player should always be able to count them. Three is the readable pack; five is the maximum for a
  single encounter; six is the set-piece. More than six is a crowd, and a crowd is comic or chaotic, not
  frightening.

### 4.3 The tell and the creak

The windup is the whole fight. It is slow — nearly a second for the sweep — and it is **audible before it is
visible**: the creak starts on the first tick of the windup and rises in pitch as the arm draws back. The
rise is the player's clock. A player who hears the rise has time to leave the arc, step off the line, or put
a shot into the elbow and cancel it.

- **Sweep:** the creak is low and long, and the arm draws across the body. The silhouette opens; the player
  can see the gap between the arm and the torso.
- **Jab:** the creak is short and high, and the arm draws straight back. The silhouette closes; the
  shoulder bundle compresses.
- **The silence after the rise:** the last quarter-second of the windup is nearly silent. That silence is
  the "now" cue. It is not a trick; it is the sound of the load settling before the strike.
- **Interruption:** a hit during the windup stops the creak mid-rise — a broken ratchet — and the arm
  drops. The player hears that they cancelled it. This is the core skill of the fight and the reason the
  creature is fair at three to one.

The head does not telegraph. It has no face; it cannot scowl, glow or open a mouth. The **body** telegraphs:
the draw, the lean, the rising creak. Keep it that way. The moment the head becomes expressive, the creature
becomes a cartoon.

### 4.4 Approach and pack behaviour

**Approach.** It does not charge. It walks, stiffly, one step at a time; the lope is the only fast variant.
The walk is a sequence of small failures caught: the knee binding slips, the hip takes the weight, the foot
lands flat with a rebar chink. It walks *at* the last disturbance, not at the player. If the player moves, it
updates; if the player stands still, it stops at the last sound and turns.

**The relay.** A pack does not coordinate. It relays. One Armature notices (head turn), and the creak of its
turn is the signal for the next; the next turns, and so on. The relay takes about 0.4 s per link. The pack
arrives one at a time, in the order it woke, like a queue. This is the pack behaviour and the anti-comedy
rule in one: **no swarm, no pile-on, no chorus**. The room turns, then the room walks, then the room tests
the load, one at a time.

This is not coordination. Free Armatures do not flank, synchronise windups or cut off a retreat; each walks
to the last sound on its own, often along the same line, and they get in each other's way. The squad
behaviour belongs to the Spindle-controlled ones (`ECOLOGY.md` §8). Free ones are meant to be underestimated;
that is the setup for the tick.

**Wake conditions.**
- **Footsteps:** 8 m radius, through walls if the wall is thin; the nearest Armature notices first.
- **Light:** 12 m radius, line of sight; a moving beam is a load moving across the cage. A steady beam holds
  one in NOTICE for up to 1 s, but every other Armature in line of sight turns toward the light. Light is a
  one-on-one tool and a pack alarm.
- **A death:** the unbind is loud; it wakes every pack within 15 m. Killing one member of a stand calls the
  rest. This is deliberate: the fight gets louder as it goes.
- **A heap stepped on:** a loose bar clatter; wakes the nearest pack within 6 m. Heaps are noisy ground.

**Breaking the relay.** Kill the Armature that is moving; the relay stops for 2 s while the others
re-settle. Break line of sight and stand still; the pack walks to the last sound and stops. A closed door
stops it (it does not open doors). A lit pool stops it at the edge (see §6). The relay is a chain of
creaks, and the player can break the chain by removing the link or the sound.

**The stand.** The set-piece pack: four to six Armatures standing in a works room among real starter cages,
scaffold bundles and bent bars. They face different directions, like a stored bundle. The player enters;
nothing moves; the room is a forest of rebar. Then one creaks, and the forest starts to turn. Use once per
stratum, not as a random spawn.

**The loper.** One in four. The same bank, a different state: the stride is longer, the torso pitched, the
arms hanging. It is a **loose bundle** — fewer tie wires, bars splayed, a rattle in the walk. HP 32 instead
of 40 (it has given up some bindings to move), speed 230. It closes distance and overshoots; it cannot turn
well. Do not give it a leap, a charge or a scream. It is a faster version of the same patient thing, and
that is worse.

### 4.5 Counterplay

- **Hear it first.** Stop and count the creaks. Idle creaks are slow, low and irregular; a rising creak is a
  windup. Three creaks is a bundle; five is a stand. The player who listens fights three; the player who
  runs in fights five.
- **Use the backlight.** Put a lit doorway or a lamp behind it. The rib cage shows light through the gaps;
  the head is a lattice; the fingers are separate bars. A real bundle is solid. The silhouette is the tell
  before the sound.
- **Read the heap.** A heap means an Armature died here. A fresh heap has bright wire ends and clean breaks;
  an old heap is rusted and settled. A cluster of heaps marks the pack's waiting place. The heap is the
  room's history and its warning.
- **Interrupt the windup.** Any hit during the draw cancels the strike and drops the arm. Aim for the elbow
  wrap (1.25×) or the knee wrap. Sustained fire on one knee wrap (8 damage) breaks it; the walk becomes a
  drag (80 u/s).
- **Step off the line, not away.** The jab is a line; a sidestep is enough. The sweep is an arc; step inside
  it, toward the body, where the arm cannot reach. Running backwards keeps the player in the arc longer.
- **Use the light deliberately.** A steady beam holds one in NOTICE, but calls the pack. Sweep the beam
  across a dark room to make the pack turn, then kill the light and move; they walk to where the light was.
- **Use the ground.** Stairs slow it (one tread at a time); water slows it to 60 u/s; a heap is noisy; a lit
  pool stops it at the edge unless it is cornered or the route is much shorter. It cannot jump, vault, climb
  or open doors. A railing is cover only if the arm cannot reach over or through it; the jab is a line, the
  sweep is an arc.
- **Do not aim for the head or the torso cage.** The head has no weak point; the torso bars deflect (0.7×).
  Shoot the joints.
- **Leave.** It does not pursue beyond the room. The fight is optional if the room has a second exit; the
  pack will not follow through a door. Boss and seal rooms are the exception; the door closes.

### 4.6 Fodder, never comic — tone rules

The office's dryness is allowed; the creature itself must never be comic. The risk with a rebar skeleton is
slapstick: the rattle, the scarecrow, the collapsing pile, the chase that clatters after the player. These
rules are binding for art, animation, sound and level placement.

1. **No scarecrow pose.** Idle arms are down, weight on both feet, head level. Never arms out, never a head
   cock, never a lean that reads as a grin. It stands like a tied cage, not a puppet.
2. **No rattle.** It does not shake, chatter, click or jitter. The idle is near-silent: one slow creak, one
   wire tick, long gaps. Comedy lives in the rattle; remove it.
3. **No slapstick death.** The unbind is top-down and heavy: wires snap, the torso folds, the limbs
   telescope, the head comes apart last, the heap settles. No springs, no bouncing bars, no head rolling, no
   comic timing.
4. **No cartoon chase.** The lope is level and wrong, not windmilling. No whoop, no clatter-run, no arms
   overhead. The pack arrives one at a time; no pile-on, no conga line, no bumping into each other.
5. **No sting.** The creature is common; the score does not hit a sting every time one notices. The creak is
   the sting. If the music acknowledges every Armature, the room becomes a haunted house.
6. **No pet behaviour.** It does not follow like a dog, tilt its head, sniff, or wait for approval. It
   tracks loads, not people.
7. **No gore, no remains.** There is no flesh, no blood, no skull, no boot, no wallet, no name tag inside
   the cage. The emptiness is the horror. A single human object in the heap would turn the creature into a
   ghost story and break the premise.
8. **No haunted armour.** It is not empty plate armour and it is not a golem. No hollow clank, no glowing
   core, no runes, no eyes. The sound is solid bars and tight wire; the light comes through the gaps, not
   out of them.
9. **Human height, human proportion, wrong material.** The scare comes from the moment the player mistakes
   it for a person and then sees the gaps. Keep the proportions exact; do not stylise the silhouette into a
   monster.

### 4.7 What it does not do

No ranged attack; no jump, vault, climb or swim; no grab; no voice; no door-opening; no spawning from heaps;
no respawn; no loot; no pack tactics beyond the relay; no fear of light (it avoids light, it is not harmed
by it); no weak point in the head; no flesh, no gore; no pursuit beyond the room; no attacking while
turning; no attack through geometry. It does not speak, and it does not pretend to be a person. It is the
one creature in the descent that is exactly what the works department says it is.

## 5. Sound palette (in words)

The palette rule: **every sound is made by a bar, a wire or a foot.** If it cannot be explained by those
three, it does not belong. No voice, no breath, no growl, no hiss, no music sting.

- **Idle:** the room's own settling, and then a single slow creak under load as the bundle takes its own
  weight — a metal door easing in its frame, low and dry, with a grain in it. A tie wire ticks as it twists.
  Long gaps. The idle is not a loop of noise; it is a rare event in a quiet room.
- **Notice:** a wire-on-wire scrape, like a chain-link lifted one link at a time; the head turn is a short,
  dry rasp; the body follows with a deeper creak.
- **Walk:** each foot is a bundle of bar ends on concrete — rebar on concrete, a thin, hard chink with a
  short ring, not a boot. The ankle wrap ticks; the knee wrap creaks; the hip takes the weight with a low
  groan. The rhythm is uneven because each leg catches. At a distance it sounds like scaffolding moving in a
  draft.
- **Lope:** the same sounds, faster, with a longer stride and a heavier landing; a loose bar drags and rings;
  the creak rhythm doubles but stays regular. It should sound like the same object, used harder.
- **Windup — the tell:** a rising creak. Think of a ratchet pulled tight, or a wire fence tightened with a
  turnbuckle: the pitch climbs as the arm draws back, the shoulder wraps ping, the body settles with a low
  shift. The sweep is low and long; the jab is short and high. The last quarter-second is nearly silent.
  That silence is the strike cue. No Halloween door creak: no slow vibrato, no wobble. The rise is a
  ratchet, not a ghost.
- **Strike:** a hard, short chock of rebar on rebar, then the bar rings. If it hits a wall, a shower of grit
  and a rattle; if it hits the player, a dull impact with a metal edge. There is no whoosh: the arm is heavy
  and slow, and the air sound is small.
- **Hit reaction:** a clang with a woody overtone, a tie-wire ping and twang, a scatter of rust; the body
  re-settles with a short creak. It sounds like a stack of bars being knocked and finding its feet again.
- **Death — the unbind:** the signature. A sequence of wire pops, high and tight and irregular, from the
  shoulders down; then the bars let go all at once — a long, descending clatter, a few bars rolling, one
  last wire twanging, then nothing. The heap settles for a second. No scream, no moan. The silence after is
  the point.
- **The heap:** a loose bar clatter when stepped on, quieter than the walk; a rare, slow creak as it
  settles. It is not silent; it is a low, occasional sound, so the player cannot tell a fresh heap from a
  living one by silence alone. The heap creaks lower than the standing body.
- **The pack:** no calls. The pack cue is multiplication: several creaks at slightly different pitches, out
  of phase, so the room sounds like a scaffold settling. Give each instance a small pitch offset (about ±3
  semitones) so the player can count them. When they all walk, the footsteps overlap into a slow, irregular
  drum. Use it sparingly; the mix should let the player count the creaks.

**How the creak telegraphs the tell.** Idle creaks are low, slow, irregular, and fall or stay level. Windup
creaks rise. The rise is the tell: it starts on the first tick of the windup and ends in the silence before
the strike. The player learns the shape of the rise the way they learn a boss's tell. If the windup is
cancelled, the rise stops mid-note — a broken ratchet — and the arm drops. The sound and the state are the
same thing; the animation should not play a separate "swing" sound over it.

**Mix notes.** The windup is a tell (high priority, near reverb, two concurrent maximum); the idle is low
priority and culled first; the death is a mid-priority event that should not be ducked by the music. The
room's ambience should duck slightly under a windup within 12 m, so the rise is never masked by a pipe or a
drip. The pack's creaks should be directional; the player locates the stand by ear before the light finds
it.

## 6. Lighting staging

The Armature is a shadow creature. It is not harmed by light and does not flee it; it prefers not to be
seen, and it is much worse to meet in the dark. Darkness does not make it stronger; it makes it
unreadable, which is enough. The level's job is to give the player just enough light to see the silhouette
and just enough dark to doubt it.

**Placement.**
- **The lit doorway.** The signature shot: one Armature standing in the dark just short of a lit doorway,
  side-on or three-quarter, so the player sees the profile against the light. The rib cage shows gaps; the
  head is a lattice; the fingers are separate bars. It does not move until the player crosses the
  threshold. Then it turns, and the light catches the bars.
- **The dark third.** Packs spawn in the darkest third of the room, among real rebar props. The lit part of
  the room is the player's, for now. The dark part is the pack's.
- **The pool it avoids.** It paths around pools of light — a floor lamp, a doorway, a grating with a light
  below. This is a pathing cost, not a wall: it will cross light if the route is much shorter or if it is
  cornered. A pool is a moat, not a shield; its arm can still reach 2.2 m into the light.
- **The edge of the beam.** In a dark room, the pack stands at the edge of the player's flashlight, just
  outside the cone. When the beam sweeps, they turn in sequence, one after another, like rebar catching the
  light. The player should feel the room paying attention before the first one steps.

**Reads.**
- **The striped shadow.** A bundle of bars casts a striped shadow; a person casts a solid one. The shadow is
  the tell at distance, before the sound. Put the light behind the creature, not in front of it.
- **The rib gaps.** The hollow torso is the difference between a person and a cage. Backlight it. A
  front-lit Armature is just rusted bars; a backlit one is a human shape with light where the chest should
  be.
- **The head.** The lattice head casts a grid shadow. From the front it is a cage with no face; from the
  side it is a cage with a jaw-like lower edge; from behind it is a bundle of vertical bars. The rear view
  is what the player sees most often when a pack follows; it must be as recognisable as the front (see §7).
- **The heap.** A heap is low and tangled; it catches light in small bright points (wire ends, bar ends) and
  casts a broken shadow. It should not read as a body. A standing Armature should not read as a heap. The
  two silhouettes must be distinct at a glance: **vertical = alive, low tangle = dead.**

**Light as a tool and a hazard.**
- A steady beam on one Armature holds it in NOTICE for up to 1 s: it faces the light and does not advance.
  This is long enough to line up a shot at the elbow or to cross a threshold. But every other Armature in
  line of sight turns toward the light, and the beam is visible from across the room. The player can freeze
  one and wake five.
- A sweeping beam is worse: it is a moving load, and the whole pack tracks it. Sweep, then kill the light
  and move; they walk to where the light was.
- Killing the light is a valid tactic. In the dark, the Armature cannot see the player any better than the
  player can see it; it hears footsteps and sees light. Stand still, let the relay stop, then move.
- **Death in light.** If the player lights the unbind, the wire snaps glint and the bars fall in a bright
  tangle. If it happens in the dark, the clatter is the only cue and the heap is a shape on the floor that
  must be lit to be understood. Do not add a scripted flash to the death; it is not a magic event.

**Room rules.**
- Every room with an Armature pack needs at least one lit doorway or lamp and one dark route. The player
  must be able to choose between the light and the dark; the creature's whole staging depends on that
  choice.
- Do not put a pack in a room with no shadow. A fully lit room reduces the Armature to a slow obstacle; use
  one, not three.
- Do not put a pack in a room with no real rebar props. The disguise needs a crowd to stand in. A bare room
  with three rebar men is a stage, not a works.
- The heaps stay. Light them or leave them dark, but do not clean them up between visits; the heap is the
  room's memory.

## 7. Sprite roles (3D artist)

**Scale and framing.** 1.8 m = **72 units**, the native enemy height; the actor's scale is 1.0, unlike the
Applicant (3.4 m). The largest pose extent from the pivot is the jab: 2.2 m of reach plus the body's lean,
about 2.5 m. Use a **5.0 m span at 768 px** (154 px/m; body about 277 px; pivot z = 0.9 m). At 512 px the
body is about 184 px and the lattice head about 25 px; the fodder should be baked at 512 first.

**One bank, one actor.** The loper is a state in the same bank, not a second actor. All Armatures share one
bank; the pitch of the creak and a small texture variant (fewer tie wires on the loper) are instance
parameters, not new sprites. The bank is paid once per actor, not per spawn; the real per-spawn costs are
AI and draw calls, so the pack should share a relay manager (one leader path, the others follow the last
creak), not six independent path searches.

**Actor and role letters.** Actor letter proposed: **R** (reinforcement). Check it against the current set
(`N` Applicant, `C` Circuit, `P` Underprint, `M` Hem, `V` Visitor, `L` Pleat, `S` Span, `J` Reunion, `A`
Ravel, `B` White Bloom) and against `package_grid.py`'s 4-character namespace before baking. Naming follows
`[actor][band][role][variant]` plus the standard frame letter and rotation.

**The sheet's poses.** The sheet supplies walk, windup, strike and hit reaction, **plus side and rear
views**. Do not re-draw the side and rear; use them. The side view sets the stride and the draw; the rear
view sets the shoulder binding pattern, which is the view the player sees most often when a pack follows.
Build to the side and rear, not just the front: the front is the least-seen angle in a chase. The rear
silhouette must read as a bundle of vertical bars with a lattice head, not as a person's back. The sheet's
side and rear are modelling references, not animation frames; the bake still needs yaw views.

| Role | Name | Samples × ticks | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | stand / idle | 2 × 12 | Perfectly still; arms down; head level. Must match the rebar prop language. |
| N | notice | 2 × 6 | Head first, body second; the wire scrape is the cue. |
| T | turn | 2 × 6 | Stiff pivot; no torso twist. |
| W | walk | 4 × 8 | The sheet's walk; the stiff gait. Four samples for the catch-and-recover. |
| L | lope | 3 × 6 | Variant; longer stride, torso pitched, arms hanging. Same bank. |
| U | windup | 4 × 8 | The sheet's windup. Two sweep and two jab variants; the last sample is the held pose before the strike. |
| S | strike | 3 × 4 | The sheet's strike. Two sweep and one jab; the arm is committed; no turn. |
| H | hit reaction | 2 × 5 | The sheet's hit reaction. A structural correction, not a flinch. |
| D | death / unbind | 5 × 6 | The sequence: wires snap, torso folds, limbs telescope, head last, heap settles. |
| X | heap / corpse | 1 | Low tangle, bright wire ends, one arm hooked over nothing. Static prop, not an actor. |
| **Total** | | **28 samples** | **1,120 PNGs at 40 views** |

**First-proof gate (11 samples, 440 PNGs):** I 1, W 2, U 2 (sweep and jab), S 2 (sweep and jab), H 1, D 2,
X 1. The gate tests the two things the encounter depends on: the stiff walk reads as a bundle in motion,
and the windup reads as a committed draw at gameplay distance. If the windup does not read, the fight is
unfair, not just ugly.

**Fodder concessions.** This is the most-spawned actor, so its bank should be the cheapest in the
bestiary. Two options, both requiring a packer check:
- **Four bands, if the packer allows a per-actor band set:** keep −45, −20, 0 and +20, and drop +45: 32
  views, 896 PNGs, a saving of about 20% of the bank. Check the stair and balcony cases first; if the player
  can look up at a pack from a lower landing, the +45 band is not optional and the saving is not available.
- **512 px first**, not 768. At 512 the body is about 184 px and the lattice head about 25 px; check the
  head still reads before approving. If the head vanishes, the creature loses its silhouette and the
  encounter loses its first scare.

At the measured 2.25 MiB per 768² PNG, the five-band bank is about 2.5 GiB resident and the four-band bank
about 2.0 GiB; at 512 px, about 1.1 GiB and 0.9 GiB. That is a bank, not a spawn cost, but it is still the
largest single line item for the most common creature.

**Bake notes.**
- **Ground contact:** the foot bundle must sit at z = 0 in every band and yaw, including the staggered HURT
  and the low death frames.
- **No breathing:** the idle must be genuinely still. Do not add a one-pixel sway or a bob; the stillness
  is the disguise.
- **The head:** no face, no eyes, no glow. The lattice must be open enough to show light through it in the
  backlit shot. If the head reads as a helmet at gameplay distance, open the lattice.
- **The death:** the heap must not clip the canvas at ±45°; check the alpha bounds of every death frame.
  The heap is a separate static prop, so the death sprite can end in a low tangle and the prop takes over.
- **The loper:** if it is a texture variant, the fewer-wire look must survive at 512 px; if it does not,
  keep the same texture and let the state carry the difference. Do not build a second bank for one variant.
